## What an empathy map is

An empathy map is a sheet with a person, or a type of person, in the middle and four boxes around them: what they **say**, **think**, **do** and **feel** about a problem. Many versions add two more, **pains** and **gains**. The idea is to put yourself in their place before you design anything for them.

**It is not a method from the book.** One account credits Dave Gray of the design firm XPLANE and says the 2010 book *Gamestorming* spread it. We did not check that against the original, so treat it as the usual story. The book does have the same exercise in other words. In Chapter 12 it asks you to put yourself in the shoes of each stakeholder, with a list of questions, and Chapter 9 is about seeing a problem through someone else's eyes. This page and tool build on those.

## The six boxes

| Box | What goes in it | Where the prompt comes from |
|---|---|---|
| **Says** | What they would say out loud about the problem, in their words | Ours |
| **Thinks** | What they would think but not say | Ours |
| **Does** | What you would see them doing | Ours |
| **Feels** | How they would feel: worried, relieved, left out | Ours |
| **Pains** | What they would see as a negative; what clashes with something else they are trying to do; what makes their life harder | The book's Chapter 12 questions |
| **Gains** | What is in it for them; why they would help, or at least not stand in the way | The book's Chapter 12 questions |

## How to fill it in

These steps are ours.

1. **Write the problem or the idea.** One line. "How can we ...?" works.
2. **Choose whose map it is.** A role or a type, such as a first-time customer or the person who approves the budget. The tool can deal you a role from the Perspective Cards. Use types, not people you know by name.
3. **Fill the four boxes first.** Say, think, do, feel. Write short lines, and write them in the person's own words where you can.
4. **Then fill pains and gains.** Ask the book's questions: what is in it for them, and what would they see as a negative?
5. **Look for gaps and clashes.** A gain that conflicts with a pain, or something they do that they do not say, is a way in.
6. **Write the ideas.** Each gap or clash is a prompt for an idea. Keep them as you would any others, then sort them.

## An example

The book's running problem is traffic in Los Angeles, and the role is the commuter, one of the four people in the Different Strokes exercise in Chapter 4. All of the entries are ours.

| Box | The commuter |
|---|---|
| Says | "I leave at six so that I do not sit in it." |
| Thinks | If I could choose when I start, I would never be in the worst of it. |
| Does | Checks the traffic on the phone before leaving, then drives the same way anyway. |
| Feels | Tense in the car and resigned about it. |
| Pains | Anything that changes my hours may clash with the school run. |
| Gains | Time back from the journey. |

Two ideas fall out of it: let people choose their start time, and show drivers where the jams are before they set off. The first comes from the gap between "I would never be in it" and the fixed start. The second comes from the checking that changes nothing.

## How true is a map?

This is our own warning. A map filled in from your head is a set of guesses about a person. That is still useful, because it shows you what you assume. It is better when you have watched real people or asked them, especially for the Does box, where what people say they do and what they do can differ. If you cannot ask, mark each line as a guess, and find out which of them are wrong before you build anything.

## Common mistakes

- **Mapping yourself.** If every box sounds like you, you are not in their shoes yet. Try a different role.
- **Mapping a crowd.** "Everyone" says and feels nothing in particular. Choose one type of person.
- **Using a real person's name.** Use a role. The book's games do, and the Perspective Cards do.
- **Stopping at the map.** The map is half the job. The ideas it gives you are the other half.
- **Filling Pains and Gains with what you want to hear.** The Chapter 12 questions are there to find the objections early. Look for the "no".

## Questions

**What is an empathy map?** A one-page sheet that shows what a person says, thinks, does and feels about a problem, so you can design with them in mind.

**Is the empathy map in the book?** No. The book's stakeholder questions in Chapter 12 and its Shoe Swapper chapter do the same job in other words, and the page says how.

**What are the four boxes?** Says, thinks, does and feels. Many versions add pains and gains, and so does this one.

**Can I print it?** Yes. Fill it in and press Print, or print it empty and fill it in by hand. You can also save a text file.

**Is what I type kept or sent anywhere?** No. It stays in your browser, and nothing is kept unless you press Save ideas to notebook.

**Who is it for?** Anyone making something for someone else: a product, a service, a meeting, a message.
