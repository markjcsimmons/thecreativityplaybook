## What SCAMPER is

SCAMPER is a set of seven prompts. You pick something that already exists, such as a product, a service, a meeting or a plan, and ask each prompt in turn. The letters stand for **S**ubstitute, **C**ombine, **A**dapt, **M**odify, **P**ut to another use, **E**liminate and **R**everse.

**This is not a method from the book.** Most sources credit the advertising man Alex Osborn, whose 1953 book *Applied Imagination* has a checklist of questions to spur ideas, and the educator Bob Eberle, who arranged similar questions into the SCAMPER mnemonic in 1971. The sources do not agree on every date, so treat those as the usual account. The book's Scavengers chapter works on the same principle: look at what is already there and change one thing about it.

## The seven questions

| Letter | The question | A small example |
|---|---|---|
| **S**ubstitute | What could you swap for a part, a material, a person or a rule? | A paper cup with a lid made of the same cardboard as the sleeve |
| **C**ombine | What could you join to it, or merge with it? | A bookshelf with a desk built in |
| **A**dapt | What else is like this? What could you copy from somewhere else? | A coat with the pocket layout of a camera bag |
| **M**odify | What if it were bigger, smaller, slower, louder or a different shape? | A tiny version of a board game you can play in a taxi |
| **P**ut to another use | Who else could use it, and for what? | An old ladder as a bookshelf |
| **E**liminate | What could you remove? What would still work without it? | A meeting with no slides |
| **R**everse | What if you did it in the opposite order, or turned it inside out? | Eating the dessert first |

The examples are ours and are only meant to show the shape of each question. Some letters have two common names. The M is sometimes written "magnify" or "minify" and the R is sometimes "rearrange".

## SCAMPER and the book

The book never names SCAMPER, but it keeps asking the same sort of question.

- **Substitute.** The book's umbrella story in Chapter 3: the old steel-ribbed umbrella has well-known bugs, and a Dutch company's lopsided umbrella swapped the round canopy for an asymmetrical one. That is the S question answered.
- **The Product Idea Generator's verbs.** The [Product Idea Generator](/tools/invention-idea-generator/) deals an object, a verb and a user. The verbs are words taken from the book's text, and several line up with the letters:

| SCAMPER letter | The generator's nearest verb |
|---|---|
| Substitute | swap |
| Combine | combine, connect, mix |
| Adapt | adapt, copy |
| Modify | shrink, stretch |
| Put to another use | none of its own (adapt comes closest) |
| Eliminate | eliminate |
| Reverse | rotate |

The matching is our reading, not the book's. The generator also has verbs with no letter, such as break, throw, hide and freeze. If SCAMPER feels too neat, those are useful too.

## How to run it

These steps are ours.

1. **Pick one thing.** A specific product, step in a process or idea. "Our onboarding email" works. "Customer experience" does not.
2. **Write what it is and what it is for.** One sentence each, so you know what must stay true.
3. **Go through the seven letters.** Spend about two minutes on each. Write every answer, even the silly ones.
4. **Ask a second question under each.** After "what could we swap?" ask "what would that let us do that we could not do before?"
5. **Circle the answers that change what it is for, not only how it looks.** A different color is a modification. A different customer is a new use.
6. **Sort what you have.** Use the book's three piles (feasible now, potential, too weird) or the [Idea Scorer](/tools/idea-scorer/).

### A walk-through

Take a coffee cup. It holds a hot drink and lets you carry it. Here are some answers, with the letters in order. All are ours.

- **Substitute:** a cup made of something you can eat.
- **Combine:** a cup with a lid that doubles as a coaster.
- **Adapt:** a cup with a handle shaped like a bicycle grip.
- **Modify:** a cup the size of a thimble for one sip.
- **Put to another use:** a cup as a pen holder, or a mini planter.
- **Eliminate:** no lid, and a cup shaped so that it will not spill.
- **Reverse:** a cup that keeps drinks cold in summer and works as a bowl when you turn it over.

Most of these are bad. A few are worth another minute, and that is how the method is meant to work.

## Common mistakes

- **Starting too wide.** SCAMPER needs a specific thing to push against.
- **Treating it as seven separate tasks.** Answers to one letter often combine with answers to another.
- **Stopping at the first answer.** The first answer under each letter is usually the obvious one.
- **Judging while you list.** The book keeps making ideas and judging them apart, and so should you. See [divergent vs convergent thinking](/techniques/divergent-vs-convergent-thinking/).
- **Skipping Reverse.** It feels odd and often produces the strangest ideas.

## Questions

**What does SCAMPER stand for?** Substitute, Combine, Adapt, Modify, Put to another use, Eliminate and Reverse. Some versions use "magnify" for the M and "rearrange" for the R.

**Is SCAMPER in the book?** No. This page is about a method from outside the book, and says where the book's own habits are similar.

**Who invented SCAMPER?** Most sources credit Alex Osborn's idea-spurring checklist as the root and Bob Eberle with the SCAMPER mnemonic, in 1971. Sources differ on some details.

**Is there a SCAMPER worksheet?** The table above is one. Write the thing you are improving at the top, then one answer per row.

**How long does it take?** The steps above take about twenty minutes for one thing. Two minutes per letter is enough to get the obvious answers out of the way.

**Is it only for products?** No. It works on a meeting, a process, a plan or a sentence. A concrete thing is easier than an abstract one.
