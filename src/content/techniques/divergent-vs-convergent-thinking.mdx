## The short answer

**Divergent thinking** opens up. You ask a question that has many possible answers, and you make as many as you can. **Convergent thinking** closes down. You take what you have and work toward the one that is best.

The book puts it in a sentence. Convergent thinking helps us converge on a single answer, such as "the answer is 42". Divergent thinking has many possible answers.

Most creative work needs both, in order. You diverge to get a big pile of options, then you converge to pick one. The trouble starts when the two happen at the same time, because judging an idea while you are still making ideas tends to kill them.

## A story from the book: the slow lifts

Imagine hotel guests are complaining that they wait too long for the lifts. The book gives two ways a manager might respond.

A manager who thinks convergently asks an engineer to fix the problem. The answer is costly new lifts. It is a sensible answer to the question "how do we make the lifts faster?"

A manager who thinks divergently asks a wider question: how do we stop the guests complaining? That opens up other answers. Give them something to do while they wait, such as magazines to read and mirrors to look in. It is a much cheaper solution, and it never touches the lifts.

The two managers had the same problem. The first was handed a single answer, and the second looked around for others. The authors add that they thought a peep show would also work, and admit that is probably just them.

## Divergent thinking

Divergent thinking is the stage where you want quantity and variety. The book's chapter on this, Idea Spaghetti, says the more ideas we come up with, the more likely it is that one or two will be very good. It also says that looking at things broadly, as children do, is at the heart of creative thinking, and that asking open-ended questions is a good way to stimulate it.

### Divergent thinking examples

- **A wider question.** The hotel manager asks how to stop the guests complaining, not how to speed up the lifts.
- **A random word.** In the Connected chapter, the authors picked the word "donation" at random and forced it onto the problem of traffic congestion in Los Angeles. In a couple of minutes they had ideas such as making drivers donate to charity every time they drive, asking people to donate their cars to charity and use other transport, a donation drive to build a better form of transport, companies sponsoring free bus passes for commuters, and employers donating one day a week of work that does not require travel. The authors say none of them is amazing, but the exercise got them past the first barrier. You can try it with the [Random Idea Generator](/tools/random-idea-generator/).
- **A target.** In the board game in the Idea Spaghetti chapter, you have five minutes to write as many ideas as a target number. The [Idea Spaghetti game](/games/idea-spaghetti/) plays it on screen.

### Divergent thinking activities

Any exercise that rewards a long list is a divergent activity, such as writing as many answers as you can to an open-ended question before a timer ends. The book's own exercises are the Idea Spaghetti sprint and the random-word exercise above.

## Convergent thinking

Convergent thinking is the stage where you narrow down. It is not the enemy of creativity. It is the second half of it. The Kill the Idea chapter says that once you have many ideas, half the job is choosing which deserve your time, money and energy, and that this is where the analytical side takes over from the creative side.

The chapter also says gut instinct plays a big role. Freddie DeMann, Evan Williams and Christian Audigier all told the authors that, when all is said and done, it comes down to a feeling for whether an idea is a big one.

### Convergent thinking examples

- **The hotel's engineer.** A single, known problem (slow lifts) with a single, known fix (install better lifts) is a convergent question. There is nothing wrong with it. It is the right tool when the answer exists and you need to find it.
- **Picking from a pile.** After a brainstorm, you cut a long list down to three, then one. The [Kill the Idea game](/games/kill-the-idea/) does this with a random finger and three saves.
- **A puzzle with one answer.** "What is 6 times 7?" has one answer and one way to be right.

## The two side by side

| | Divergent thinking | Convergent thinking |
|---|---|---|
| The question | Open-ended, many answers | Closed, one best answer |
| The aim | Quantity and variety | Quality and a decision |
| The rule | No idea is a bad idea | Judge, compare, choose |
| Used when | You need options | You have options and need to pick |
| The book's tool | Idea Spaghetti | Kill the Idea |

## Groups and individuals

The book adds one more point. It cites evidence that groups are better at convergent thinking, while individuals are better at divergent thinking. When a problem has a single best answer, a group gets there more effectively than people on their own. When many different ideas are needed, a group tends to produce more clichéd and traditional ones than individuals do. The study the book cites is by Leigh Thompson, in *Academy of Management Executive*, 2003.

The authors draw a blunt conclusion: group brainstorms are not always worthwhile, and a bad one can leave people frustrated and confused. One reason is that groups try to avoid conflict, and wildly different ideas are often in conflict with one another.

Our suggestion, not the book's, is to use that finding in the order of the work. Let people make ideas alone first, then bring the group together to share, build on each other's ideas and choose. The [brainstorming template](/templates/brainstorming-template/) is built that way.

## When to use each

A simple rule of thumb, ours rather than the book's:

1. **Use divergent thinking when the answer is not known.** If you are asking "what could we do?", you need options before you can decide.
2. **Use convergent thinking when the options are on the table.** If you are asking "which one?", stop making ideas and decide.
3. **Do not mix them in the same minute.** Set a time for each, say out loud which one you are in, and switch on purpose.
4. **Expect to go back and forth.** After choosing, you may need to diverge again on how to make the chosen idea work, then converge again on a plan.

## Common mistakes

- **Judging while you make ideas.** The rule "No idea is a bad idea" exists because criticism kills ideas before they have had a chance to breathe.
- **Stopping at the first answer.** The hotel manager who hires an engineer has solved the problem as stated. The cheaper answer was available to the one who asked a wider question.
- **Never converging.** A big pile of ideas is not a result. The book calls it a plate of spaghetti without a knife and fork.
- **Brainstorming only as a group.** The evidence in the book says individuals do better at making ideas.
- **Treating gut feeling as the enemy.** The Kill the Idea chapter says experienced people rely on it when they choose.

## Try it: two steps, 20 minutes

1. **Diverge (10 minutes).** Pick a real problem and write it as an open-ended question. Play the [Idea Spaghetti game](/games/idea-spaghetti/) with it, or use the [Random Idea Generator](/tools/random-idea-generator/) for prompts, and write down as many ideas as you can. Do not cross anything out.
2. **Converge (10 minutes).** Copy your best ideas, up to 19, into the [Kill the Idea game](/games/kill-the-idea/) and play it through. The one that survives is your starting point.

## Questions

**Is divergent thinking the same as creativity?** No. It is one part of it, the part that makes options. Choosing among them, and making the chosen one work, is the other part.

**Who came up with the two terms?** The book does not say, so we do not either. It uses them as everyday ideas, with the hotel example from a training article it credits in a footnote.

**Which is better?** Neither. They do different jobs. Divergent thinking is better when the answer is not known, and convergent thinking is better when you have to decide.

**Can one person do both?** Yes. Do them in separate sittings if you can, so you are not tempted to judge while you are still making ideas.
