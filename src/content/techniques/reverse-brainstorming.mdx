## How it works

Normal brainstorming asks, "How can we reach this goal?" Reverse brainstorming asks the opposite: "How could we make sure we fail?" You list every way you can think of to make the goal worse, and the sillier the better. Then you go down the list and turn each bad idea round into a fix.

**It is not a method from the book.** The nearest thing in the book is the Kill the Idea game in Chapter 11, where a random idea is about to be killed and you have three saves. That game attacks an idea. Reverse brainstorming attacks the goal, and uses the attack to find ideas.

## How to do it by hand

These steps are ours.

1. **Write the goal as a question.** "How can we ...?" The [asking better questions](/guides/asking-better-questions/) guide explains why.
2. **Reverse it.** "What is the surest way to make this worse?" Write it where everyone can see it.
3. **List the bad ideas.** Set a timer for three minutes. Make them extreme. If the list dries up, ask what would make it slower, more expensive, more confusing or impossible to start.
4. **Flip each one.** Read the bad idea aloud, then ask what would stop it, or what the opposite would be. Write the fix beside it.
5. **Keep the fixes you like.** They are ideas like any others, so sort them with the book's three piles or the [Idea Scorer](/tools/idea-scorer/).

### An example

The book's running problem is traffic in Los Angeles. The goal: "How can people in Los Angeles spend less time stuck in traffic?" The bad ideas and fixes here are ours.

| Way to make it worse | The flip |
|---|---|
| Make sure everyone has to arrive at the same time. | Let people start at different times, or work without traveling one day a week. |
| Make sure every car carries one person. | Fill the cars: car pooling, or shared minibuses. |
| Hide where the jams are until drivers are in them. | Show drivers where the jams are before they set off. |
| Make the journey as dull as possible. | Give people something to listen to on the way. |

Two of those fixes echo ideas the book itself arrives at for the same problem, one of them with a car pool lane and one with a day away from travel. Reversing the goal reached the same neighborhood by a different road.

## Why it can be easier than a normal brainstorm

This is our reading, not the book's. People often find it easier to list ways to make something fail than ways to make it succeed. There is nothing to defend, and the bad ideas are funny. That can loosen up a group that is being too polite. The Idea Spaghetti chapter makes a related point: the "perfect brainstorm" removes judgement so that ideas can come out. A list of silly bad ideas is a safe way to get started.

## Where it fits

- **You are stuck.** The list of bad ideas is quick to start and breaks the silence.
- **The team is too polite.** Everyone can criticize, because the thing being criticized is imaginary.
- **You want to see risks.** The bad list is also a list of things that could go wrong, and you can check whether any are already happening. (This is a way of using the list, not a separate method.)
- **You have a goal but no first idea.** The flips give you the first few.

## Common mistakes

- **Flipping too literally.** "Slow" to "fast" is not an idea. Ask what would make it fast.
- **Stopping at the bad list.** The list is half of the method.
- **Making the bad ideas too believable.** If everyone would nod at an item, push it further until it is silly.
- **Treating every flip as a decision.** A flipped idea is an idea. It still has to pass the same test as any other.
- **Letting one person write the list.** Several people produce stranger bad ideas than one.

## Questions

**What is reverse brainstorming?** A brainstorming method that starts from the opposite of your goal. You list ways to make it fail, then flip each one into a fix.

**Is reverse brainstorming in the book?** No. The Kill the Idea game in Chapter 11 is the nearest thing, and the page says how they differ.

**What is an example of reverse brainstorming?** "How can we make people spend more time stuck in traffic?" gives answers such as making everyone arrive at once, and each flips into a fix. The table above has four.

**How is it different from normal brainstorming?** Normal brainstorming asks for ideas that reach the goal. This asks for ideas that wreck it, then reverses them. Both end in a list of ideas to sort.

**Is it the same as a pre-mortem?** They overlap, because both imagine failure. This page is about the brainstorming method only.

**Is my list sent anywhere?** No. It stays in your browser, and nothing is kept unless you press Save to notebook.
