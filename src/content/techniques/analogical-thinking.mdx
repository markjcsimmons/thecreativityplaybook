## What analogical thinking is

An analogy is a comparison between two things that are different but share something. Analogical thinking uses it to solve a problem. You find something that your problem resembles, work out how that thing is handled, and carry the answer back.

The book does not use the term, or "combinational creativity", a name used outside the book for making new ideas by combining existing ones. Its Brain Flash chapter calls the move a **simile** ("my problem is like ...") or a **metaphor** (treating the problem as if it were about something else). The chapter's argument is that making comparisons helps highlight similarities and differences, and sparks ideas that might help. The stronger the picture in your head, the richer the comparison.

The book's own examples are a camera that is like an eye and an umbrella that is like a canopy of leaves. Its question for your own work is: what is your business challenge similar to? Does it remind you of something in nature or from another field?

## Five examples from the book

### 1. A satellite on a string

The chapter's first story is from NASA. Engineers were designing a satellite tethered to a space station by a very long wire. When they tried to reel it in, it swung out in ever-wider arcs, which took far too long and put stress on the wire.

They saw that a satellite on a tether is like a person with a yo-yo on a string. The yo-yo comes back because it spins and winds the string up. So instead of the space station winding the satellite in, a small motor on the satellite could pull the satellite to the station, and the wild swinging stopped. The book cites an academic article for the story, and we have not checked it independently.

### 2. A potato chip in a tube

Packed loosely in a bag, potato chips take up a lot of room. Packed tighter, they break. The book says researchers at Procter and Gamble thought about the similarities between chips and leaves. Dry leaves break apart easily, but leaves pressed together while moist and then left to dry have no gaps and do not break. That suggested a way to pack them: the chemist on the project developed a foil-lined tube with a resealable lid that holds the chips tightly together, so they do not break when moved around. It is how Pringles are sold.

### 3. A traffic jam as clogged arteries

The book's running problem is traffic congestion in Los Angeles. Think of the city as a patient with clogged arteries. Now there is a picture, and you can ask what a doctor would do. Surgery suggests a new road to bypass the blockage. A healthier diet suggests preventing the clog in the first place, by cutting down on accidents or the number of cars.

### 4. A traffic jam that is not a mountain stream

The chapter also tries the opposite: what is the problem unlike? Traffic in Los Angeles is unlike a mountain stream, which is clean, fast and peaceful. Making traffic more like a stream suggests cleaner cars, keeping the flow moving and noise dampening around the roads. A stream flows in one direction, which suggests one-way roads. The authors suggest sitting by a mountain stream in your mind, or drawing it or finding a picture, and thinking about how to recreate that feeling for your problem.

### 5. Bakers and bread

The last example is a method for metaphors. Write the problem as a simple question: "How can people in LA spend less time in their cars?" Pick something to stand for it, such as baking, and rewrite the question: "How can bakers spend less time making bread?" Then picture the baker and list what he might do:

- make bigger batches
- sell less bread
- make bigger loaves
- outsource the baking
- bring in assistants
- close the bakery

Each answer is a metaphor for the traffic problem. Bigger batches suggest bigger groups traveling together, in buses or car pools. Closing the bakery suggests closing some roads to make people travel less. Perhaps they could earn their bread without going to the office so much.

| Borrow from nature | What in nature does this resemble? | Chips and leaves |
| Picture it | What would I see if it were something else? | Clogged arteries |
| Find what it is unlike | What is the opposite of my problem? | The mountain stream |
| Swap the subject | How would this question look for a baker? | Bread and traffic |

## How to do it

The book gives the examples. These steps are ours.

1. **Write the problem as a question.** "How can we ...?" The [asking better questions](/guides/asking-better-questions/) guide explains why.
2. **Pick a source.** Use something you know well, a picture, a thing from nature or another field. You can let the [Brain Flash game](/games/brain-flash/) deal you a picture.
3. **Finish the sentence** "My problem is like ... because ...". Write several versions, each with a different reason.
4. **Ask how the source works.** What does it do? What are its parts? What does it do when it goes wrong, and what fixes it?
5. **Carry each answer back.** Translate it into your problem, as the baker's answers became answers about traffic.
6. **Repeat with a second and third source.** One comparison gives a few ideas. A different one gives different ideas.
7. **Check the comparison before you trust it.** See below.

### Check the comparison

An analogy can suggest an idea, but it cannot prove the idea works. This list is ours, not the book's.

- **Where does it break down?** A traffic jam is not really an artery. Ask what is different about your problem before you copy a solution.
- **Is the idea still good without the comparison?** State it in plain words. If it only sounds convincing as a metaphor, it needs more work.
- **Did you borrow the cause, or only the look?** The yo-yo worked because of how winding pulls an object back. A matching shape alone would not have been enough.
- **Who has tried it?** If you think of an idea from another field, look at whether it has actually been used in your field.

## Common mistakes

- **Stopping at the first comparison.** The first is usually the most obvious.
- **Picking a source too close to your problem.** Another company in your industry gives you a competitor's idea. The book's examples come from space, leaves and baking.
- **Treating the comparison as proof.** It is a way to get ideas. You still need to test them.
- **Using it as decoration.** A good analogy changes what you do. If it only makes the report sound better, it has not helped.
- **Skipping the translation step.** The ideas about bakers only help once you map them back to traffic.

## Questions

**What is analogical thinking?** Solving a problem by comparing it to something else and using what works there. The book teaches it as similes and metaphors.

**What are examples of analogical thinking?** The book's examples are the satellite as a yo-yo, potato chips as leaves, traffic as clogged arteries and traffic as a bakery. Pages elsewhere give business examples, such as a firm borrowing a method from another industry, but they are outside the book.

**Is analogical thinking the same as lateral thinking?** They overlap, because both look for ideas away from the obvious. The [lateral thinking](/lateral-thinking/) hub explains how the book's habits fit that term.

**Do I need to know a lot about the source?** You need to know it well enough to ask how it works. That is why the book's sources are everyday things such as a yo-yo or a bakery.

**How is it different from the random word technique?** The random word technique deals you something you did not choose and makes you find a link. Analogical thinking starts from what your problem is like, and you choose the source.
