## What the five whys is

You state a problem. You ask why it happened and write down the answer. Then you ask why about that answer, and again, until you reach a cause that you can do something about. Five is a rule of thumb. Sometimes three is enough, and sometimes it takes more.

**It is not a method from the book.** It is associated with Toyota, where it is usually credited to Sakichi Toyoda, founder of Toyota Industries, and described by the engineer Taiichi Ohno. Sources disagree on who first used it, so treat that as the usual account. It was built for finding the cause of something that went wrong, and most guides to it are about quality control and operations.

## How to do it

These steps are ours.

1. **State the problem as a fact.** "The monthly report went out late." Not "We are bad at reports."
2. **Ask why, and write the answer.** Answer with something you know, not something you assume. If you do not know, find out before you go on.
3. **Ask why about that answer.** Write it under the first.
4. **Repeat until you reach something you can fix.** You will know it when the answer is a rule, a habit or a missing step, not a person's mistake.
5. **Fix that, then check.** Fix the last answer in the chain, and see whether the first problem stops.

### An example

A made-up office problem, all ours.

1. **The monthly report went out late.** Why? The last section arrived two days late.
2. **Why did the last section arrive late?** The person writing it was waiting for the figures.
3. **Why were the figures late?** They are only updated on the third of the month.
4. **Why does the report deadline come before the third?** Nobody asked when the figures would be ready when the deadline was set.
5. **Why did nobody ask?** The deadline was copied from last year's calendar.

The cause is a missing check when the deadline is set. The first answers blamed the writer, and that would have fixed nothing. The fix is to agree a deadline with whoever owns the figures.

## Where it goes wrong

Limits to know about:

- **It follows one chain.** Many problems have several causes. If you ask why about only the first answer, you miss the others. When an answer has two causes, ask why about both.
- **It stops at a symptom.** "Because we ran out of time" is not a cause. Ask why you ran out.
- **It can turn into blame.** If the last answer is a person's name, you have probably stopped too early.
- **Guessing.** An answer nobody checked is a story, not a cause.
- **Five is not magic.** Stop when you reach something you can change.

The method is also best for problems that went wrong, such as a late report or a broken process. It tells you less about a problem with no clear failure, such as "how do we get more customers?" For that, reframing the question usually works better.

## Five whys and the book's WHY

The book's Chapter 4 game, What's Your Problem?, uses the question "WHY?" too, but for a different job. You write your problem as "How can we ...?", and then you roll a die to decide which question to ask about it: how, why, which, when, who or what. Each answer gives you a new angle, and you rewrite the problem at the end.

Its example is a band that wants to sell more music. Asked WHY people are spending less on buying music, the answer is that they can get it free online. That points the problem at making the things you sell better than what people get for free. Its WHY is one of the six questions, used to widen the problem.

| | The five whys | The book's game |
|---|---|---|
| Asks | The same question again and again | Six different questions |
| Moves | Down, towards a cause | Sideways, towards a better question |
| Starts with | Something that went wrong | A goal written as "How can we ...?" |
| Ends with | A cause to fix | A problem restated in a new way |

You can use both. Use the five whys to find what is really causing the problem, then write the cause as "How can we ...?" and use the book's questions to open it up. The comparison is ours.

## Questions

**What is the five whys technique?** A way to find the cause of a problem by asking why about the problem, and then why about each answer, until you reach something you can fix.

**Do you always need five?** No. Five is a rule of thumb. Stop when you reach a cause you can change.

**Is the five whys in the book?** No. The book's What's Your Problem? game asks "why" once, as one of six questions, to reframe a problem.

**Who invented the five whys?** It is associated with Toyota. It is usually credited to Sakichi Toyoda and said to have been described by Taiichi Ohno, but sources disagree on who first used it.

**What is an example of the five whys?** The late monthly report above: the writer was late because the figures were late, which came from a deadline set without asking when the figures would be ready.

**Is it a creativity technique?** It is a way of finding causes more than of making ideas. It helps creative work by making sure you are solving the right problem.
