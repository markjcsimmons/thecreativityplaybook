## What it is

The Connected chapter says creativity is about making connections between two things that seem unrelated. The random word technique is the simplest way to force that: take your problem, take a word you did not choose, and make them meet.

The book puts it under the heading chaos theory. A word you did not pick cannot be a word that fits your problem, which is exactly why it works. It makes you think in a direction you would not have taken.

Outside the book the technique is also called random stimulus, and the idea of making a forced link is sometimes called forced association. The book uses neither name.

## How the book does it

1. **Pick a word at random.** The authors grabbed a dictionary, flicked through the pages and put an ink-stained finger on one.
2. **Force a connection** between the word and the problem.
3. **Set a short time.** The authors gave it a couple of minutes.

Their problem was traffic congestion in Los Angeles. Their word was "donation". They admit it was a hard one, and they thought about trying again, then decided that would be cheating, because it would no longer be random. They went with it. Here is what they came up with:

- Drivers could be made to donate to charity every time they drive.
- People could be asked to donate their cars to charity and use other means of transport.
- There could be a donation drive to build a better form of transport.
- Companies could be encouraged to sponsor free bus passes to donate to commuters.
- Employers could be asked to donate one day a week of employees' time to work that does not require them to travel.

The authors do not claim these are amazing. What struck them was that it worked: once you get past the initial barrier of thinking there is no connection between your problem and the word, you can force yourself to come up with ideas, and some may be worth pursuing.

### A caution from the authors

The book notes that the word they picked may not have been so random after all. Others on that page of the dictionary, such as "donkey", "door" and "doodle", they thought would be non-starters, and perhaps their subconscious filtered those out. They decide it does not matter much. The point of the exercise is to introduce stimuli that seem unrelated to the problem, and if the subconscious is pushing in a certain direction, perhaps that is fine.

## Other ways to get a random stimulus

The book's "Joker" section lists everyday sources of a random prompt. Each time, you look at it and ask how it connects to your problem. Paraphrased:

- **The fridge.** Pick a couple of items. Look at the packaging, the design and the ingredients. When did you last use it? How does it make you feel? What is good and bad about it? How does it relate to your problem?
- **The television.** Watch five channels for a minute or two each, and try to make what you see and hear relevant to your project.
- **A newspaper.** Pick two or three articles without thinking too much, read the first paragraphs and ask how the stories connect to yours.
- **A magazine.** Look at the ads and pictures. Why do they look the way they do, and what do they try to say? What ideas do they spark?
- **A library or bookshop.** Pick three books from sections you would not normally visit, and spend 10 or 15 minutes with each. Think about how the author would approach your project, and what the title of a book about it would be.

The book also lists a random web page. We leave that out because the site it names no longer exists, but any random web page works the same way.

## A single word, looked at a new way

The director Matthew Warchus told the authors about a time early in his career when he was working on Shakespeare's *Much Ado About Nothing*. A character speaks of the "career of his affections", and it was being played as the progress of a man's affections from bachelor to married man. Looking the word up in rehearsal, Warchus found that when the play was written "career" meant sharp and sudden changes of direction. A horse and cart careering across the road is the sense that survives today. The scene changed.

It is not the random word technique exactly, since he chose the word. But it shows the same idea: one word, seen a different way, can open up a problem.

## A related technique: cut-ups

The Connected chapter opens with the cut-up technique, which the novelist William S. Burroughs developed in the late 1950s and early 1960s. You cut text into small pieces and rearrange them at random. The book describes David Bowie writing lyrics by writing phrases on strips of paper and picking them out at random, and says Kurt Cobain and Thom Yorke used the method too. The authors say they are not necessarily advising anyone to give up control, but that a degree of chance can be a wonderful thing in business.

## How to run it

These steps are ours, built on the book's example.

1. **Write the problem as a question.** "How can we ...?" works. The [asking better questions](/guides/asking-better-questions/) guide explains why.
2. **Pick a word you did not choose.** Use a dictionary, or the [Random Idea Generator](/tools/random-idea-generator/), which deals words from the book's lists.
3. **Take what you get.** The authors' rule. No re-picking.
4. **Set a timer for two minutes** and write every idea that links the word to the problem, however thin the link.
5. **Do not judge.** Judging comes after.
6. **Repeat with two or three more words.** Then look across the lists for the ideas that keep coming back.
7. **Choose.** The [Kill the Idea game](/games/kill-the-idea/) helps you cut a long list.

## Common mistakes

- **Re-picking until the word suits.** That removes the whole point.
- **Stopping at "there is no link".** The authors say the skill is getting past that barrier.
- **Judging as you go.** The ideas can be thin. Collect them first.
- **Using it as a decision tool.** It makes ideas. It does not tell you which one to pursue.
- **Using it too early.** The book gives randomness as a nudge for stale thinking, not a replacement for understanding the problem.

## Questions

**Does the random word technique work?** The authors say it did for them: they got five ideas in a couple of minutes from a word that looked unhelpful, and some might be worth pursuing. The book does not claim more than that.

**How do I pick a random word?** Open a dictionary at a random page and point. A random page from a book works too. The key is that you do not choose.

**What if the word is useless?** Use it anyway, as the authors did.

**What is the difference from the Random Idea Generator?** The [Random Idea Generator](/tools/random-idea-generator/) deals two random things and asks what they could be together. The random word technique deals one word and asks how it connects to your problem.

**Can a group do it?** Yes. Give everyone the same word, write alone for two minutes, then compare. The [brainstorming games](/exercises/brainstorming-games/) page has group formats.
