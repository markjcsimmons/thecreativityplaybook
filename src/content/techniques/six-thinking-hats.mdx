## What the six thinking hats is

You and your group pick a problem or a decision. Then you look at it through six kinds of thinking, one after another. Everyone uses the same kind at the same time, so nobody is arguing for the idea while someone else is arguing against it. Each kind has a color of hat:

| Hat | The kind of thinking | The question it asks |
|---|---|---|
| **White** | Facts and information | What do we know? What do we still need to find out? |
| **Red** | Feelings and hunches, with no need to explain them | How does this feel? What is your gut saying? |
| **Black** | Caution and risk | What could go wrong? Why might this not work? |
| **Yellow** | Benefits and value | What is good about this? Why might it work? |
| **Green** | New ideas and alternatives | What else could we do? What if ...? |
| **Blue** | Managing the thinking itself | What are we here to do? Which hat next? What have we decided? |

**This is not a method from the book.** It was introduced by Edward de Bono in a 1985 book called *Six Thinking Hats*. The descriptions above are ours, in our own words, and this page is not affiliated with him or with anyone who trains people in the method. If you want the method as he wrote it, read his book.

## How to run a session

These steps are ours.

1. **Write the question.** One line. "Should we take this forward?" or "How can we ...?" The blue hat's first job is to agree it.
2. **Choose the hats and their order.** You do not have to use all six. One order that works for a new idea is blue, white, green, yellow, black, red, blue. When a decision has already been made and you want to test it, start with red, then black and yellow.
3. **Give each hat a few minutes.** Announce the hat, say what it means, then everyone thinks that way until time is up. Two to five minutes each is a sensible start.
4. **Park off-hat remarks.** If someone raises a risk during yellow, write it down and keep it for black.
5. **Close with blue.** Read back what came up, say what you will do next, and who will do it.

If you are on your own, it works too. Write the question at the top of a page, then write under each hat in turn.

### A worked example

The book's running problem is traffic in Los Angeles. One idea it arrives at is employers letting staff spend a day away from travel. The group's question is "Should we take this idea forward?" Everything below is ours, to show the shape.

- **Blue (open).** The goal is to decide whether to try it. The order is white, green, yellow, black, red, then blue.
- **White.** Which jobs could be done away from the office? Which could not? We do not yet know how many people would use it, so that is something to find out.
- **Green.** A fixed day for everyone. A day each person chooses. A trial in one department. A day that rotates so that the office is never empty.
- **Yellow.** Fewer people on the road at the busiest time. People get the journey back. It could make the company a more attractive place to work.
- **Black.** Some jobs need someone on site. Managers may worry about seeing their teams. If everyone picks the same day, the office is empty that day and the roads are no better on the others.
- **Red.** Some people would love it. Others would feel cut off from the team.
- **Blue (close).** The yellow and black lists point the same way: try it in one team before deciding for everyone. One person finds out which jobs qualify.

Notice how the black hat's worries became part of the plan instead of a reason to stop. That is the point of the method.

## The hats and the book

The book does not teach the hats, but two parts of it do the same job.

**The gang of five.** Chapter 9 pictures a gang of five children, each with a different way of approaching a problem: considering everyone's feelings, thinking very broadly, wanting a solution quickly, wanting to explore every avenue, and wanting something nobody has tried. The book says to imagine how each would handle your problem, because that helps you switch into different modes of solving it. The hats are the same trick with six modes. Our loose pairing, which is only a reading:

| The book's child | The nearest hat |
|---|---|
| Considers everyone's feelings | Red |
| Explores every avenue | White, which gathers everything known |
| Wants something nobody has tried | Green |

The other two, one who wants a solution quickly and one who thinks very broadly, have no clean match. The children are five people. The hats are six modes that everyone takes up in turn.

**Making, then choosing.** Chapter 11 says to make ideas first and judge them second, and sorts them into three piles: feasible now, potential and too weird. The green hat is the making and the black hat is the judging. The hats method puts them in different rounds, so the criticism does not arrive while the ideas are still fragile.

## Common mistakes

- **Wearing two hats at once.** "This is great, but it will never work" is yellow and black in one breath. Say one, park the other.
- **Starting with black.** Criticism that arrives first kills the ideas that have not been made yet.
- **Leaving out blue.** Without anyone watching the clock and the question, the session drifts back into an ordinary argument.
- **Asking red to explain itself.** The red hat is for a reaction. Asking for reasons turns it into white.
- **Treating the hats as personality types.** In the usual account they are modes that everyone puts on and takes off, not labels for people.
- **Using all six every time.** A small decision may need only a few.

## Questions

**What are the six thinking hats?** Six kinds of thinking, each with a color: white for facts, red for feelings, black for caution, yellow for benefits, green for new ideas and blue for managing the process.

**Who created the six thinking hats?** Edward de Bono, in a 1985 book of the same name. This page is our own short explanation and is not his text.

**Is it in the book?** No. The book's gang of five children in Chapter 9 and its split between making and choosing ideas in Chapter 11 do similar jobs, and the page sets them side by side.

**What order should the hats go in?** There is no single order. For a new idea, blue, white, green, yellow, black, red, blue is one that works. Start and end with blue.

**Which hat is for brainstorming?** Green. It is the hat for new ideas, and it works best before black.

**Can I do it alone?** Yes. Write the question, then write under each hat in turn.

**How long does it take?** At two to five minutes a hat, a full run takes roughly half an hour. That is our estimate, not a rule.
