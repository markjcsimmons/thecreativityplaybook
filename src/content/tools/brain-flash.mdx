## How to play

1. **Decide on a problem.** For example, "How do we improve customer service?"
2. **Throw the dice.** The first die counts along the bottom of the board and the second counts up the side. That square holds a picture.
3. **Compare your problem to the picture.** Complete the sentence: "It is like ... because ...". Then write the ideas the comparison sparks.
4. **Pick your best idea.** You play five rounds. The book gives a point for the best idea in each round. Playing alone, you keep your best idea from each round.

## Why it works

The Brain Flash chapter says that visual stimuli help generate new ideas. The book's explanation is that pictures tap the creative side of the brain without being inhibited by the side that uses language to process thought. We treat that as a way of talking, not as anatomy. The practical point is the same: if you ask your mind to find connections between a problem and a picture, you can end up with insights you would not have found by thinking about the problem directly.

The picture does not have to relate to the problem, and that is the point. The comparison is forced, and forcing it is what produces the ideas. In the book's example, a picture of juggling skittles became a simile about acrobats and speed. The same picture could just as well have become a simile about coordination, and that would have led to different ideas.

## The pictures

The board has 36 pictures, from a rocket to a conch shell. They are the book's own illustrations. We left out two for a workplace site, so there are 34 here. If your dice land on a missing square, the game throws again, and no picture comes up twice in one game.

## Where this comes from

This is the Brain Flash board game from the Brain Flash chapter, played on screen. The rules, the example and the pictures are the book's. Nothing you type leaves your browser and nothing is stored.

## Questions

**What if the picture does not suggest anything?** Say what it is for, what it is made of, or what it does. A candle burns down. A rope holds things together. Any of those can become a simile.

**Is there a right simile?** No. Several can come from the same picture, and each leads to different ideas.

**Can I use my own problem?** That is the idea. Use anything you want ideas for.
