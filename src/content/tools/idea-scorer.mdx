## How to use it

1. **List your ideas.** One per line, two to thirty. Or use Pick from your notebook to bring in ideas you saved earlier.
2. **Set the weights.** For each criterion, say how much it matters to you: ignore it, or give it a low, medium or high weight. Effort and cost count against an idea.
3. **Score each idea from 1 to 5.** The scale for each criterion is written beside it. If you cannot tell yet, leave it blank. A blank is left out, not counted as zero.
4. **Read the ranking.** Each idea gets a score out of 100, a rank, and a place on the impact and effort chart.
5. **Argue with it.** If the top idea feels wrong, ask why. Either a score is off or a weight is.
6. **Save the winners.** Save to notebook marks the top ideas as shortlisted, with their score in a note. From there you can send them to [Kill the Idea](/games/kill-the-idea/) or [Blast Off](/games/blast-off/).

## What the six criteria mean

| Criterion | The question | 1 means | 5 means |
|---|---|---|---|
| Impact | How much difference would it make to the people it is for? | Hardly noticed | A big difference |
| Effort | How much work is a first version? | A few hours | A team for months |
| Cost | How much money does a first version need? | Almost nothing | A large budget |
| Speed | How soon could you try a first version? | More than a year | This week |
| Fun | How much do you want to work on it? | A chore | Cannot wait |
| Fit | How well does it fit what you or your team are trying to do? | Barely | Right at the center |

Effort and cost are scored as "how much", so a 5 counts against an idea. The other four are scored as "how good", so a 5 counts for it. The scorer turns them all into one 0 to 100 scale.

## How the score is worked out

The score is a weighted average of the numbers you gave. Each criterion you scored is turned into a number from 0 to 100 (with effort and cost flipped so that less is better), then averaged using your weights. A criterion you left blank, or gave no weight, is left out. If there is nothing to average, the idea has no score.

That is all it does. It does not know your market and it cannot predict whether an idea will work. It ranks what you told it, and it is only as good as your judgments. Scoring the same ideas twice, a week apart, is a good way to find out how much to trust them.

## Reading the chart

The chart puts impact up the side and effort along the bottom, and it is a common way to sort ideas. The labels are ours.

- **Quick wins** (high impact, low effort) are the first place to look.
- **Big bets** (high impact, high effort) may be worth it, but plan them.
- **Fill-ins** (low impact, low effort) are cheap but small.
- **Time sinks** (low impact, high effort) are the ones to let go.

A 3 is the middle, so an idea that scores 3 on either axis is marked "in between" and not pushed into a corner. The ranking table lists the same scores in words, for anyone who cannot read the chart.

## Where this fits with the book

The Kill the Idea chapter says to make ideas first and judge them second. The judging is the job of the analytical half of the brain, and the book's simplest method is to sort ideas into three piles: those that are feasible now, those that have potential, and those that are just too weird. Even the weird ones are noted for later and not thrown away. The scorer gives you a different tool for the same job, with numbers. Use whichever suits you, or both.

The chapter also says gut instinct plays a big part in whether an idea is a big one. That is a good check on any score. If the scorer's top idea leaves you cold, that tells you something the numbers did not.

For ideas that look feasible, the chapter suggests writing them up as "What I see myself doing is ..." and listing what is great about the idea, spin-offs and your practical concerns. That works well after scoring.

## Questions

**Is this from the book?** No. The book's method for choosing is the three piles and the Kill the Idea game. The scorer, its criteria and its chart are ours.

**What is a good score?** There is no pass mark. Scores are for comparing your ideas with each other. A 70 means it did better than a 50 on your own criteria, not that it will succeed.

**Why do effort and cost count against an idea?** Because a high number means more work or more money. The scorer flips them so that a lower number is better.

**Can I use it with a team?** Yes. Have everyone score alone, then compare, because people often disagree and the disagreement is useful. The tool scores one set of numbers, so enter an agreed number, or run it once per person.

**Is my list sent anywhere?** No. It stays in your browser, and nothing is kept unless you press Save to notebook.
