## How to use it

1. **Write your problem.** A sentence, or a question that starts "How can we ...?". You can pick one you saved in your notebook.
2. **Choose the deck.** All 55 cards are in by default. Take out a group if it does not suit your problem.
3. **Flip a card.** You get a role, a way of thinking or a set of conditions, with a question to start you off.
4. **Think as them.** Write ideas the way they would approach the problem, not the way you would. Two minutes is plenty. The timer is optional.
5. **Keep the ideas and flip another.** Four or five cards is a good session. No card comes up twice until you have seen the whole deck.
6. **Compare.** Which ideas surprised you? Which would one of the other people reject, and could you change the idea so they would not?

## What is in the deck

| Group | Cards | What they do |
|---|---|---|
| Fresh eyes | 15 | People who know little about your problem, so they are not held back by what has been tried |
| People with a stake | 14 | People who care about the outcome, each for a different reason |
| Ways of thinking | 10 | Different habits of mind: quick, thorough, careful, bold |
| Different conditions | 16 | The same problem with different limits of time, money and scale |

Every card is a role or a type. There are no named people in the deck.

## Where the cards come from

**Fresh eyes.** The Shoe Swapper chapter says we are held back by what we know, and that the best way round it is to ask "inexpert witnesses". They are people who do not know what has been tried, such as people from other departments, roofers, writers, musicians, sushi chefs and jockeys, and children, whose view the book calls naive by definition. A passenger in a cab is another of the book's examples. Those cards are marked "From the book". The rest are our own in the same spirit.

**People with a stake.** The Chapter 4 exercise Different Strokes has a mayor, a police chief, an environmentalist and a commuter look at the same traffic problem. All of them agree it is a bad one, but they care for different reasons, and the reason decides which solution is best. Those four cards are the book's. The others, such as the customer, the supplier and the person who approves the budget, are ours.

**Ways of thinking.** The same chapter pictures a gang of five children, each with a different way of approaching a problem: considering everyone's feelings, thinking very broadly, wanting a solution quickly, wanting to explore every avenue, and wanting something nobody has tried. The first five cards in this group are those five. The book says each has something to offer, and that imagining how each would handle your problem helps you switch into different modes of solving it. The last five in the group are ours.

**Different conditions.** These are all ours. They change the limits: no money, unlimited money, one hour, ten years, a motor-racing pit crew, a touring band's road crew. The Far Out chapter's advice that obvious solutions take you nowhere new is the reason to try them.

## Role storming and the book

Outside the book, taking on someone else's identity to make ideas is called role storming. The book does not use the name. Its two closest exercises are the [Shoe Swapper game](/games/shoe-swapper/), where the dice give you a pair of shoes to think in, and Different Strokes, where each member of a small team gets a role with a name and, if you like, a prop. The cards combine the two: you choose the problem, the deck deals the person.

We make no claim that role storming gives you more ideas than other methods. The book does not, either. What it says is that looking through someone else's eyes can lead to ideas that are not held back by old thinking.

## With a team

1. **Write the problem on a wall or a screen** where everyone can see it.
2. **Press "Deal 4 cards for a team".** Each person gets one card.
3. **Two minutes alone.** Everyone writes ideas as their card would, on paper.
4. **Read them out.** Add the ideas to the box, one per line.
5. **Swap.** Deal again and pass the cards round, so each person gets a new point of view.
6. **Look for the ideas that more than one card would agree on.** The book suggests asking which person would agree with each idea and how to reconcile the ones who would not.

The [brainstorming games](/exercises/brainstorming-games/) page has more group formats.

## Common mistakes

- **Writing your own ideas under a new name.** Think as the card would, and your ideas will change.
- **Dismissing a card as silly.** The book's point is that the link looks thin at first. Get past that.
- **Stopping at one card.** One viewpoint gives you a handful of ideas. Several give you a spread.
- **Using it to guess what real people want.** An imagined customer is not a real one. If an idea matters, ask a real person.

## Questions

**Is this from the book?** In part. The cards marked "From the book" come from Chapters 4 and 9. The tool, the other cards and the question on every card are ours.

**What is role storming?** A brainstorming method in which people take on another identity. The book's versions are Shoe Swapper and Different Strokes.

**Are the cards real people?** No. Every card is a role or a type, never a named person.

**Can I make my own cards?** Not yet. If you have a viewpoint that works well, type it into the problem box and treat it as the card.

**Is my problem sent anywhere?** No. It stays in your browser, and nothing is kept unless you press Save ideas to notebook.
