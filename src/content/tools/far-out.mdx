## How to play

1. **Write your ideas.** List as many uses for a paperclip as you can, one per line. There is no timer. The longer your chain, the better.
2. **Press Done.** The game now shows you the book's list of ideas that people commonly come up with.
3. **Tick the ones on the list.** For each of your ideas, tick it if it is on the book's list, or close enough. Each tick moves you one space. Each idea that is not on the list moves you two.
4. **See how far you got.** Your ideas become a chain of paperclips. You are playing against yourself, so the book says everyone is a winner.

## Why it works

The Far Out chapter is about how thinking big can trigger interesting ideas. The man in the chapter started with one red paperclip and, after a series of trades, ended up with a three-bedroom house. The chapter says that when we are trying to come up with creative solutions, we should use whatever tricks we can to fire up the imagination, and exaggeration is one of them, because it takes us away from the more obvious solutions.

This game puts that into practice. The ideas people think of first are the ones on the list, and they earn the least. The ones further out earn more.

## What counts as being on the list

The book's list has 25 ideas, from a key-ring to an emergency wedding ring. The book does not say how close a match has to be, so you decide. Be honest, because the chain is only useful to you if you are. If you are not sure, ask whether most people would have thought of it.

## Where this comes from

This is the Far Out board game from the Far Out chapter, played on screen. The rules and the list are the book's. This version does not draw a board. It counts your spaces. Nothing you type leaves your browser and nothing is stored.

## Questions

**Can I use a different object?** The game is about the paperclip, but the idea works with a brick or a coat hanger. The [Random Idea Generator](/tools/random-idea-generator/) can give you an object.

**Why is the list hidden until I finish?** Because seeing the common ideas first would push you toward them.

**Should I stop at one idea per line?** Yes. One idea per line makes the count honest.
