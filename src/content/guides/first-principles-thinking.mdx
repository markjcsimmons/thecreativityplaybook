## What first principles thinking is

Most of the time we solve problems by analogy. We look at how it has been done before and change a little. First principles thinking asks you to stop there and go lower. What do you know is true about this problem, whatever anyone has done before? Then you build an answer up from those facts.

It has three moves:

1. **List the assumptions** you are making.
2. **Test each one,** and keep only what you can show is true.
3. **Rebuild** an answer from what is left.

**It is not a method from the book, and the book never uses the term.** Sources trace the phrase to Aristotle, whose first principles were the starting points that a field of knowledge is built on. They say that the modern "rebuild it from scratch" use is a later adaptation of his words, and that in business it is often linked to Elon Musk. We do not rely on either account, and we do not use the stories that usually go with them.

## The first half is in the book

Chapter 4 of the book opens with the first two moves. List the assumptions you are making about the situation, then throw out the ones that do not hold true. Its example is traffic in Los Angeles. People assume that they need cars to get around, and that they need to travel around Los Angeles at all. The authors test each. Do people need cars? They have legs, and there are buses, bicycles and the occasional train. Do they need to travel? Some journeys are essential, but many are for eating out, going to a movie or going to the beach.

The book also offers a trick: flip an assumption to its exact opposite and see whether it changes anything. If it makes a real difference, keep it in. If it does not, you may be able to drop it.

What the book does not do is the third move, a rebuild from the basic facts. That is the part this page adds, and it is ours.

## How to do it

These steps are ours, built on the book's first two.

1. **Write the problem as a question.** "How can we ...?"
2. **List what everyone takes for granted.** Rules, habits and "we have always". Write them as plain statements, such as "The meeting has to be weekly."
3. **Test each one.** Ask three things: is it true, how do we know, and what would change if it were not? Mark each as a fact, a guess or a habit.
4. **Find the need underneath.** For every habit, ask what it is for. That gives you the real requirement.
5. **Rebuild from the needs.** Write what you would do if you were starting today with only the facts and the needs.
6. **Compare with what you do now.** The gaps are your ideas. Sort them as you would any others.

### A worked example

A made-up one, all ours: a weekly team meeting that people dislike.

| What we take for granted | Fact, guess or habit? | What it is for |
|---|---|---|
| It has to be weekly | Habit | Keeping people up to date |
| It lasts an hour | Habit | Time for news and for decisions |
| Everyone has to be there | Guess | Making sure decisions are heard |
| It has to be in person | Habit | Feeling like a team |

Rebuilt from the needs: keep people up to date with a short written note on Fridays. Hold a half-hour meeting every two weeks, only for decisions, and invite the people each decision touches. Give the team feeling its own time, such as a coffee break with no agenda. Not every habit has to go. The point is that each one now has to earn its place.

### The book's problem

The same method on the book's own traffic problem, with the book's assumptions and our rebuild.

- **Taken for granted:** people need cars, and people need to travel.
- **Tested:** both are partly true. Some journeys need a car, and some are for things that could come to you or happen closer to home.
- **The need underneath:** people need to reach work, food and entertainment, and how they get there is a choice.
- **Rebuilt:** bring some of those to people (work from home for a day, local shops and services), and make the rest of the journey easier (car pooling, shared minibuses).

The book arrives at ideas of this kind itself. This is another route to them.

## Limits

- **It is slow.** Use it on the few problems where the accepted answer looks wrong, and use analogy for the rest.
- **Your facts may be assumptions in disguise.** "We know customers want X" is only a fact if you have asked them.
- **You cannot go down forever.** Stop at things you can check, or at things you have to accept.
- **A rebuild can reinvent the wheel.** Some old answers exist because they work. If the existing answer survives the test, keep it.
- **It does not make ideas by itself.** It clears the ground. You still need a way to generate ideas to build on, such as the book's games.

## First principles and the five whys

Both go down, but they go down for different reasons.

| | First principles | The five whys |
|---|---|---|
| Asks | What is true, and what is only habit? | Why did this happen? |
| Goes down to | The basic facts and needs | A cause that you can fix |
| Best for | Building something new | Finding what went wrong |
| Ends with | A rebuilt answer | A fixed cause |

## Questions

**What is first principles thinking?** Breaking a problem down to what you know is true, then building an answer from those facts instead of copying how it was done before.

**Is first principles thinking in the book?** Not by name. Chapter 4 teaches listing your assumptions and testing them, which is the first half. The rebuild on this page is ours.

**Where does the term come from?** Sources trace it to Aristotle, and say the modern use is a later adaptation. In business it is often linked to Elon Musk. We do not rely on either.

**What is an example of first principles thinking?** The book's own case is the Los Angeles traffic problem, where it tests the assumptions that people need cars and need to travel. The worked example above takes a weekly meeting through the same steps.

**Is it better than reasoning by analogy?** Not always. Analogy is quick and often right. First principles is worth the effort when the accepted answer looks wrong.

**How is it different from the five whys?** The five whys looks for the cause of something that went wrong. First principles looks for what is true so that you can build something new.
