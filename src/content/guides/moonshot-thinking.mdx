## What this page means by moonshot thinking

A moonshot is a goal so large that it looks nearly impossible, and moonshot thinking is the habit of aiming at one on purpose. The name comes from the space race, and the book tells that story itself. It does not use the word moonshot.

The book's name for the habit is thinking BIG. Chapter 8, Far Out, opens with the tagline "Think BIG and don't let the truth get in the way of a good story", which is a joke about exaggeration, not advice to mislead. The chapter then teaches three ways to think bigger on purpose. This page follows them, and marks what is ours.

Phrases such as "big hairy audacious goals" come from business writing outside the book. The book does not use them, and we do not rely on them.

## The book's case: a bigger problem gets bigger ideas

The chapter's argument is about mindsets. We tend to think in proportion to the size of the problem: if it looks small we think small, if it looks big we think a little bigger, and if it is a crisis we apply all our creative energy to it. So one way to get bigger ideas is to make the problem look bigger.

The book says people tend to think of more innovative solutions when given a bigger problem. It stops your thinking being in small increments and lets you dream up ideas that are very different in nature and scale. Small, safe ideas are what you get when the goal is a small improvement.

### An example from the space race

The book's example is the moon. In May 1961, it says, President Kennedy announced to the nation that the United States had to win the space race against the Soviets or lose its place as the world's dominant nation. He had created a crisis. On 20 July 1969, just over eight years later, the United States became the first nation to put men on the moon.

The book quotes Kennedy to a joint session of Congress. As the book gives it, he acknowledged the Soviet head start and said that nobody could guarantee being first, but that any failure to make the effort would guarantee being last. The point for creative work is that the goal was not a small step. It was a large, vivid goal that people could picture, and the work was organized around it.

This is the book's account of the story. We use it as an illustration of the habit and do not make claims about the space program itself.

## Three ways to think bigger

### 1. Dramatize the problem

The chapter says dramatizing a problem can force us to think a little bigger. It shows how the right words change how seriously a problem is taken. The book asks: how can you dramatize your own challenge so that you focus your creative energy in the right direction? How could you bring it to life to inspire yourself and other people to solve it?

### 2. Exaggerate it

The authors say exaggerating a scenario to an extreme is a great way to get creative juices flowing. They take their running example, too much traffic in Los Angeles, and push it one step further: the cars are absolutely stationary. Nothing is moving. People cannot get to work or home.

A solution to that exaggerated problem might be to quit using cars altogether. The authors admit it may be impractical. But the notion can free up ideas for dramatically reducing the number of cars on the road, ideas you might not have thought of otherwise. The advice is to go for the outlandish idea and then work on ways to make it practical. Obvious solutions are too similar to how things are now to take you anywhere new.

### 3. Play a game with wild ideas

The book offers two games.

**Mad World.** A small group gets ten minutes to write the most unlikely solution they can think of on a card, one idea per card. Everybody then adds notes to the others' cards to make the crazy ideas more practical. Each player has two votes for the ideas they think are still the most implausible after the additions. The craziest idea wins, which rewards outlandish thinking. The authors point out that along the way, the ideas that did not win might have real potential. In their example, a crazy idea of getting everyone to fly led to short shuttle flights between local airports.

**Superheroes.** Give people the powers of made-up superheroes, such as the power to make things very big, to double things or to move them anywhere instantly. Then apply the power to the problem. In the traffic example, the power to move things instantly suggests taking cars out of the city, and the book follows it back to real life: perhaps only certain kinds of cars should be allowed on the roads. The power to double suggests doubling the buses. The power to raise things off the ground suggests raised rail systems.

## Looking ten years ahead

The Scavengers chapter has a related technique called ball-gazing. The authors say that looking only five years ahead tends to anchor our thinking in the present, and we assume the world will be much like it is now. Looking at least ten years ahead makes it easier to ignore short-term trends. They then throw in disruptions that might have happened in between and ask what life would be like.

Their list is silly on purpose. It includes children being in charge and a discovery that lets people read each other's thoughts. The purpose is to loosen your thinking, not to predict. A moonshot goal and a ten-year look are close relatives: both ask what you would do if the usual limits were not there.

## Thinking small is also a tool

Not every problem needs a moonshot. A footnote in the chapter offers the opposite exercise: shrink the problem until it is very, very tiny. Then the solutions become ways to make the most of the situation, not ways to change it radically. The example is a small delay in traffic: a radio station for people stuck in traffic, or short self-improvement podcasts to make the time useful.

Pushing a problem up or down is a way to see it differently. Try both.

## A three-step method

The steps are ours, built on the book's examples.

1. **State the big version.** Write the goal as if limits did not exist. If the problem is too many cars, write "no cars". If it is a slow sales process, write "customers buy the moment they hear about it". Make it vivid and a little silly.
2. **Write wild ideas for ten minutes.** Aim for quantity. Do not judge. Use the [Far Out game](/games/far-out/) or the [Random Idea Generator](/tools/random-idea-generator/) if words dry up.
3. **Add the practical step.** Take the two wildest ideas and ask what the smallest real version would be. The Mad World game's "make it more practical" step does this. The shuttle flights came from asking how a crazy idea could work.

Then choose what to try. The [Kill the Idea game](/games/kill-the-idea/) helps with that.

## The first step

A big goal needs a way in. Kyle MacDonald's One Red Paperclip project is the chapter's example. He began on 12 July 2005 with a single red paperclip, advertised it online and offered to trade it for something bigger or better. After 14 trades he ended up with a house in Kipling, Saskatchewan. His lesson, told to the authors:

> If you don't take the first step, you'll never go anywhere.

The book does not claim that any goal is possible. Its point is that the project was big, simple to describe and started with one small step. See [storytelling in business](/guides/storytelling-in-business/) for how a story like that spreads.

## Common mistakes

- **Confusing big with vague.** "Change the world" is not a goal. The book's examples are specific: no cars, a house for a paperclip.
- **Stopping at the wild idea.** The Mad World game only works because other players make the crazy ideas practical.
- **Treating exaggeration as a claim.** The book's tagline is a joke. In business, what you tell people has to be true.
- **Skipping the first step.** An idea with no first step is a slogan.
- **Using it for every problem.** The book also shows shrinking a problem. Choose the direction that frees ideas.

## Questions

**What is moonshot thinking?** Setting a goal far larger than the problem seems to need, then working back to a first step. The book teaches the habit as thinking BIG, and it uses the moon landing as one of its examples.

**Does the book use the word moonshot?** No. It uses the Apollo story, but the word and the idea of moonshot projects at companies come from elsewhere, and this page does not cover them.

**What are big hairy audacious goals?** The phrase comes from business writing outside the book. It describes the same kind of large, vivid goal.

**Is moonshot thinking only for big companies?** The book's examples are a man with a paperclip, a city's traffic and a café. The habit is about the size of the goal, not the size of the organization.

**Does a moonshot have to be realistic?** The book says the opposite at first: go for the outlandish idea, then work on ways to make it practical.
