## What intrapreneurship is

An entrepreneur starts a business. An **intrapreneur** works inside one, and behaves like an entrepreneur: they spot an opportunity, build a case for it, gather help and push it out the door, with the company's money and people behind them and the company's rules around them. The practice is **intrapreneurship**.

**The word is not from the book.** Sources credit Gifford Pinchot III and Elizabeth Pinchot with it, from a 1978 white paper and their 1985 book *Intrapreneuring*, though the history of the word is debated, so treat that as the usual account. The book does not use it. It does have the idea in another form, which the next section sets out.

## What the book says

The book never says "intrapreneur", but it touches the idea in three places.

- **Internal entrepreneurs.** In its section on hiring people who go against the grain, Chapter 12 cites the music-industry deal-maker Danny Socolof on "internal entrepreneurs", who drive change in large companies by taking risks, sometimes without permission.
- **The company that prefers efficiency.** The 2010 Introduction tells the story of 3M, which was famed for its creativity and, as the book tells it, had begun to put efficiency first. Art Fry, who invented the Post-it note, questioned whether his idea would have seen the light of day in a company that preferred efficiency to experiment. This is the 2010 account, and the company has changed since, so read it as a story about the tension. The book is not describing how 3M works now.
- **How to launch.** Most of Chapter 12 is about getting an idea to launch. It says that taking an idea from vision to action takes perseverance and self-belief, and that it helps to be surrounded by great people. It asks you to list the people who could help you, and to put yourself in the shoes of each stakeholder.

That last one is the useful part for an intrapreneur. The book's questions for each stakeholder are:

- What is in it for them?
- Why would they be interested in helping, or at least not standing in the way?
- What would they see as a negative?
- Does it conflict with other things they are trying to do?
- Does it make their life more difficult?

Inside a company, your stakeholders are your boss, finance, the legal team, the people who would have to do the work, and the person whose budget you would borrow. These are the questions that find the "no" before you meet it.

## Examples

The book's own example is the Post-it note and the question Art Fry asked about it. Other people cite further examples, such as Lockheed's Skunk Works, Sony's PlayStation and Google's 20% time. We did not check those, so look them up before you rely on them.

Examples are always a little unfair, because they are the ones that worked, and that is why they can look easy.

## What it takes

This list is ours.

- **An idea that matters to the company,** not only to you. It has to solve something they care about.
- **A small first step.** A prototype, a pilot or a one-page plan costs less to say yes to than a project.
- **Allies.** Find who will help before you ask who will approve.
- **A good pitch.** A short, clear case for why. See [elevator pitch examples](/guides/elevator-pitch-examples/).
- **Staying power.** The book calls it perseverance and self-belief. You will hear no more than once.
- **A way to take no.** The book's Kill the Idea chapter is about fighting for the ideas worth it and letting the rest go.

## How to start

These steps are ours, built on the book's launch chapter.

1. **Choose a problem the company already has.** Not "my idea", but "our problem".
2. **Write the idea on one page.** What it is, who it is for, what the first step is and what it needs. The book's one-page launch plan is a good shape for it.
3. **List the stakeholders and ask the book's five questions about each.** Write down the answers. Notice who loses something.
4. **Find two allies before you find a sponsor.** People who will say "yes, that is a problem" in a room you are not in.
5. **Ask for something small.** A month, a budget you can name, one customer, one team. A small yes is easier to give.
6. **Show something.** A sketch, a mock-up or a trial beats a talk.
7. **Report back.** Tell the people who helped what happened, including when it did not work.

## Risks

- **Working without permission is a risk, not a method.** The book notes that internal entrepreneurs sometimes take risks without it. We are not suggesting you break your company's rules, and what is acceptable depends on your company and your role. Ask when in doubt.
- **The idea may not be wanted.** Not every company has room for it, and the answer may be no for reasons that have nothing to do with the idea.
- **You may carry it alone.** Without allies, an idea that depends on one person ends when they move on.

## Common mistakes

- **Pitching the idea before the problem.** Start with what is wrong now.
- **Asking for the whole budget.** Ask for the first step.
- **Skipping the people who say no.** Meet them early and ask the stakeholder questions.
- **Going quiet after the pitch.** Keep the allies informed.
- **Waiting for permission to think.** Making ideas needs no approval. Launching them does.

## Questions

**What is intrapreneurship?** Acting like an entrepreneur inside a company you work for: spotting an opportunity and getting it launched with the company's resources.

**What is an intrapreneur?** An employee who does that. The word is a mix of "intra", meaning inside, and "entrepreneur".

**Is intrapreneurship in the book?** The word is not, but Chapter 12 is about launching an idea with other people, and it mentions "internal entrepreneurs". The steps on this page are ours.

**Who coined the term?** Sources credit Gifford Pinchot III and Elizabeth Pinchot, in 1978 and 1985, though the history of the word is debated.

**What is an example?** The book's own is the Post-it note, which Art Fry invented at 3M and wondered whether the company would back. Other people cite more.

**How is it different from entrepreneurship?** An entrepreneur starts and owns a business and carries the risk. An intrapreneur works inside one, with its resources, and the company carries most of the risk.
