## What this page means by whole brain thinking

Search for "whole brain thinking" and most of what you find is a commercial model, with a test that sorts people into four thinking styles. This page is not about that. It follows the book's Left Brain, Meet Mr Right chapter, which makes a simpler point: when you work on a problem you can come at it in two ways, and the best results come from using both.

**A note on left and right.** The book talks about the two halves of the brain and gives them personalities. The popular idea that a person is "left-brained" or "right-brained" is a myth, and a strict split of the brain into a logical half and a creative half is not how it works. We use "left" and "right" the way the book does, as a figure of speech for two modes of thinking: analysis and insight.

## Two ways to solve a problem

The chapter describes two routes to an answer.

**Analysis.** You work through it step by step, by trial and error. The book's example is a word puzzle. You are given three words and have to find a fourth that goes with all of them. Take *cheese*, *ocean* and *sky*. You might try "skylight", then test it against the other two words, and find it does not work.

**Insight.** The answer arrives whole, from nowhere, with what people almost always describe as an "A-ha!" feeling. The answer to the puzzle above is *blue*: blue cheese, blue ocean, blue sky.

Try one more. The words are *light*, *birthday* and *stick*. The answer is at the end of the next paragraph.

The book's point is that neither route is the right one. People solve these puzzles both ways, and the same person switches between them. (The answer is *candle*: candlelight, birthday candle, candlestick.)

## What the book's studies say

The chapter backs its argument with research. The details below are the book's account, from studies published between 2006 and 2008. We have not checked them against the original papers.

**A short distraction helped.** In an experiment at the University of Amsterdam, students were given creative tasks: inventing names for pastas, naming Dutch places that start with a given letter, and thinking of uses for a brick. Some had three minutes to think before answering, some answered at once, and some spent three minutes on a dull task that kept their attention busy, clicking whenever a circle on the screen changed color. The distracted group gave more original answers. The researchers' explanation, as the book tells it, is that conscious thought stays under a searchlight while unconscious thought ventures into the dark nooks and crannies of the mind, and that the dull task freed the unconscious to do its work.

**People switch between analysis and insight.** The book cites brain-scan studies that asked people to solve the word puzzles and say whether the answer came with an "A-ha!". The scans looked different when the solution came by insight, even before the answer arrived, and even before the problem was shown. But no one used only one method. Each person used both, at different times, and switched between them. The book guesses they might have been trying another approach, or giving one part of the brain a rest.

**Some people lean towards insight.** A follow-up study the book cites found that resting brain activity differed between people who tend to solve problems by insight and people who do not. So some people are more creative than others, the book says, but all of us can switch into a creative mode, and we can train our brains to do it. It borrows a line from Louis Pasteur: chance favors only the prepared mind.

**Mood matters, in both directions.** The chapter reports a study in which people in a happy mood did well on creative tasks but not so well on other tasks. That is a reason to want both modes. A mood that helps you make ideas may not help you check them.

The director Matthew Warchus gives a practical version in the chapter. When he works with an assistant, he asks them not to help if he is stuck, because being stuck comes just before a really brilliant idea. He calls a room full of people who are all stuck a good sign.

## Making and choosing, as two teammates

The book's Kill the Idea chapter goes furthest with the metaphor. It pictures the two halves as teammates. The right half has played a good game making ideas, and its energy is low. The left half's turn comes: it takes the field and analyzes the ideas to see which are practical. The two pass the baton as they pass each other, with a "well done" from one and a "good luck" from the other.

The left half's skill, the chapter says, is analyzing ideas to see which have a chance of succeeding. Its simplest method is to sort the ideas into three groups: feasible now, with potential, and just too weird. Even the weird ones should be noted for later, maybe for another project.

Read the metaphor without the anatomy and the advice is practical: make ideas first, judge them second, and do not mix the two jobs. That is the same distinction the [divergent vs convergent thinking](/techniques/divergent-vs-convergent-thinking/) page covers.

Other chapters make the same point in their own words:

- **Brainstorms.** The Idea Spaghetti chapter says a brainstorm needs some form of "meditative zoning out" so that the analytical side does not interfere before ideas have been properly formed. An informal setting helps.
- **Responding to ideas.** The Idea Commune chapter says the analytical side is often too keen to start editing and thinking of practicalities before the creative side has had a proper chance. Its fix is to start by saying "What I like about this idea is ..." and name at least one good thing about it.
- **Pictures.** The Brain Flash chapter says language is processed largely in the analytical half and visualization in the creative half, and that sketches and similes can find connections without language getting in the way. The book's explanation is a figure of speech, but the practice works: draw it, or compare it to a picture.
- **Jokes.** The Connected chapter says a joke or a provocative image thrown into a stuck discussion is a nudge telling the analytical side to step back for a while and letting the freewheeling side take a shot.

## A routine that uses both modes

The book gives the pieces, not a routine. This one is ours, so change it as you like.

1. **Analyze first (10 minutes).** Write the problem plainly. List what you know and what you are assuming. The [creative problem solving](/guides/creative-problem-solving/) guide shows how.
2. **Make ideas without judging (10 minutes).** Aim for quantity. Use a picture or a random prompt if words dry up. The [Brain Flash game](/games/brain-flash/) does the picture part.
3. **Rest the problem (10 minutes or more).** Do something absorbing and unrelated. The [Wheel of Distraction](/games/wheel-of-distraction/) picks one for you.
4. **Come back and write down what surfaced.** Anything, however small.
5. **Choose (10 minutes).** Sort the ideas into feasible now, potential and too weird. Then play [Kill the Idea](/games/kill-the-idea/) on the shortlist.

## Common mistakes

- **Deciding you are one type.** The book's studies found people used both methods and switched between them.
- **Judging while you make ideas.** The analytical side is quick to edit. Make it wait.
- **Never resting the problem.** The Amsterdam study is the book's case for stepping away.
- **Treating the metaphor as anatomy.** The halves are a way of talking about two modes.
- **Using only the exciting mode.** An idea with no analysis behind it is a story, not a plan.

## Questions

**Is whole brain thinking the same as the Herrmann model?** No. That is a commercial model with its own test. This page uses the book's idea of two modes of problem solving that a person switches between.

**Is there such a thing as a left-brained or right-brained person?** The popular idea is a myth. The book's research found that individuals use both analysis and insight, though some lean towards one.

**How do I get more insight?** The book does not offer a formula. Its advice is to work on the problem, step away to something absorbing and unrelated, and return. The Amsterdam study is its evidence.

**Does music help?** The book says listening to certain music can enhance creativity, and that a good mood helps creative tasks. It also notes that a good mood did less well on other kinds of task.

**Why does a page about analysis mention jokes?** The book says humor and creativity are closely related, because both make surprising connections between two ideas. It uses a joke as a nudge for a stuck discussion.
