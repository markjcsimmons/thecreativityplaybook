## Which kind of visualization this is

The phrase means three different things on the web. This page is about only one of them.

- **Data visualization.** Charts, plots and dashboards that show numbers. Not covered here.
- **Mental imagery.** Picturing yourself reaching a goal. Not the book's subject either, though one of its methods asks you to picture a vision in detail.
- **Visual thinking.** Putting a problem on paper, or in a photo or a comparison, so that you can see it differently. This is the book's Chapter 6, and this page.

## Why the book asks you to draw

The chapter starts from one claim: when people have to rely on words alone, it is hard to restructure a problem and see new ways of looking at it. A picture can be an actual sketch, or a verbal one such as a simile or a metaphor. Either lets you think more creatively than a paragraph of description does.

The chapter gives four ways to do it. They are in order below, from the one that needs a pad of paper to the one that needs only a sentence.

## 1. The before-and-after sketch

This is the chapter's main method. It asks for a large pad of paper and pens or pencils in several colors. The steps below follow the book, in our words.

1. **Settle in.** Clear your mind of everything else, including the meeting that was canceled and the presentation that is due. Then choose the creative problem you want to solve.
2. **Sketch how things are now,** on the left half of the page. Draw only the elements that carry your thoughts about the situation, and add short labels if that helps. The sketch is for you, not for anyone else.
3. **Sketch how you want things to be,** on the right half. This is your vision for success, and it should differ a lot from the left. You can exaggerate. The book's example is traffic in Los Angeles: draw the opposite of jams and snarl-ups, perhaps no cars at all, or no roads, only green fields, parks and trees. Take time over it so that it is vivid.
4. **Ask what the barriers are** between how things are and how you want them to be. Perhaps it is the design of the product, or too few people knowing about the service, or poor communication between departments. Sketch each barrier in a different color, and label it.
5. **Sketch ways to break the barriers down,** in a third color.
6. **Take a breather.** The book says the visual side of the brain needs time to absorb what you have laid out. Step away, then come back and look. Ideas tend to arrive as you look at the page.
7. **Pin it up.** Put the sketch where you can see it and add to it as ideas come.

The book is blunt about what the sketch is for: you, and you alone. If it later seems a useful way to explain something to others, make a simple diagram from it. It is not a presentation.

### The book's traffic example, in words

Because you cannot see a drawing here, this is the same sketch as a list. The picture of the present is ours, and the vision is the book's. The barriers and ideas are ours.

- **Now:** a motorway full of cars, every car with one person in it, and a clock on the dashboard.
- **Vision:** no cars on the roads, just green fields, parks and trees.
- **Barriers:** people have to get to work, and there is no good alternative way to travel.
- **Ideas through them:** work without traveling one day a week, share cars, and give people a better way to travel.

## 2. A photo and a title

Dave Stewart describes a method that starts with a photograph. He often takes it of a person, but it can be of anything at all, such as an empty room or a bookcase. What matters is that you have frozen a moment in time. Then you look at the picture and give it a title, as if it were the poster for a film.

The title is where the ideas start. Talk about what it could mean, and each meaning is a different story. He says the method has led him to a song and a film idea from a single photo. Then he turns it on a business problem. Take a picture, ask how it relates to the business, and give it a title that does. His made-up example is a tennis ball company: a photo given the title "Game, set and match", with a spoof web page behind it. He is plain about it. He is not saying that the result is a good idea, only that it shows how an idea can come about through visualization.

To try it, take a photo of anything near you, write three possible titles, and write what each would mean for your problem.

## 3. The collage

The chapter's group method is a collage. Split the problem into as many parts as there are people, which the book says is usually between four and eight, and give each person a part to sketch a solution to. Everyone shows their sketch, and the others add ideas to improve it, with no heckling, in the spirit of positive collaboration. The sketches are then combined into one picture that may trigger new ideas.

The book's example splits the Los Angeles traffic problem into seven parts, such as too many cars, people driving alone and accidents causing delays. The [brainstorming template](/templates/brainstorming-template/) has a short version of the exercise.

## 4. Similes and metaphors

The fourth kind of picture is verbal. You describe your problem as something else: "My problem is like ... because ..." The book's examples include a camera that is like an eye and an umbrella that is like a canopy of leaves. The [Brain Flash game](/games/brain-flash/) turns this into play by dealing you a picture to compare your problem to.

## Tips

These are ours.

- **Draw badly on purpose.** A rough sketch is enough, and the book says the sketch is for you.
- **Use more than one color.** The method uses a color for each job, so you can see which lines are the problem and which are the ideas.
- **Label things.** A few words stop you from forgetting what a squiggle was.
- **Keep the pad large.** Small paper makes small drawings.
- **Leave it alone for a while** and come back to it, as the book advises.

## Common mistakes

- **Drawing the solution first.** Start with how things are now.
- **Making the vision timid.** The method works by contrast, so make the two halves very different.
- **Treating it as an art task.** Nobody is judging the drawing.
- **Showing it to the boss for praise.** The book jokes that this will not go as you hope.
- **Stopping at the pictures.** Write down the ideas the sketch gave you, then sort them.

## Questions

**What are visualization techniques?** On this page, ways of drawing, photographing or picturing a problem to see it differently: a before-and-after sketch, a photo with a title, a group collage and similes.

**Is this about data visualization?** No. Charts and dashboards are a different subject. This page is about visual thinking for ideas.

**Is this the same as creative visualization?** Not quite. Creative visualization usually means picturing a goal in your mind. The book's method puts the picture on paper and then asks about the barriers.

**Do I need to be able to draw?** No. The sketch is for you and nobody else, so stick figures and boxes will do.

**How long does the sketch take?** The book gives no time. Our estimate is 20 to 30 minutes for the drawing, then some time away from it.

**Can a group do it?** Yes, as a collage. Split the problem into parts, one per person, and combine the sketches.
