## What a problem statement is

A problem statement says what you are trying to fix, for whom, and why it matters, in a sentence or two. It does not say how to fix it. The wording matters more than it looks, because the book's main point in this chapter is that the question you ask shapes the answers you get.

The book recalls that when Albert Einstein got home from school, his mother asked him not what grades he got, but whether he had asked any good questions. Its version of a problem statement is a question that starts "How can we ...?" It keeps it simple and clear, and it leaves room for more than one answer. The examples below show why the wording is worth your time.

## Example 1: one traffic problem, four statements

The chapter's running example is traffic congestion in Los Angeles. The authors say the point is not to solve it, but to show how to think through a problem you can relate to. Here is how the wording changes the direction.

| The statement | What it points to |
|---|---|
| How can we reduce traffic congestion in Los Angeles? | Better roads and better public transport |
| How can we make travel around Los Angeles easier? | The same, with a focus on the commute itself |
| How can we reduce the need to travel around Los Angeles? | Working from home, local services, fewer journeys |
| How can we get people to use their cars less? | Taxes and fees, or incentives to leave the car at home |

The first two assume that people need to travel. The authors challenge that assumption. They point out that many journeys are for things like eating out, going to a movie or going to the beach, and that it might be better to reduce the need to travel than to make travel easier. The book adds that if you only switch people to public transport, you are dealing with the effect of the problem and not its cause.

The book also gives a real-world example of the fourth wording. In 2006 the city of Seoul launched a "No-Driving Day" scheme that gave drivers incentives to leave their cars at home one day a week. That is a 2010-edition example, so read it as a story about a way of framing the problem, not as a report on how Seoul manages traffic today.

## Example 2: a band and its music

The book's board game, What's Your Problem?, has a worked example about a band. A band starts with "How can we sell more of our music?" That wording assumes that selling music is the best way to make money from music. A band that asks "How can we make money from our music?" has not made that assumption, and the answers change. One of them might be using the recordings to promote live gigs rather than trying to sell them.

The two questions differ by a few words, and they point to different businesses.

## Example 3: a company that asked what business it was in

The chapter opens with the story of Nokia. When Jorma Ollila became the company's CEO in 1992, the business was a mix of paper products, rubber goods and cables. He asked what business Nokia should be in, and his answer was mobile communications. The book credits that question with the company's transformation.

This is the 2010 book's snapshot. Nokia's fortunes have changed since, so use it for what it shows about questions, not as a picture of the company now. What it shows is that the most valuable problem statement can be about the whole business: not "how do we sell more toilet paper?" but "what should we be doing at all?"

## Example 4: the same problem, four viewpoints

In the Different Strokes exercise, the authors look at the traffic problem as four different people would. Each statement below is ours, based on the viewpoints the book describes.

- **The mayor:** how can we reduce the time office workers lose to traffic, because it is hurting businesses across the city?
- **The police chief:** how can we reduce the volume of traffic that causes accidents and puts a strain on police resources?
- **The environmentalist:** how can we reduce the exhaust fumes that are poisoning the air?
- **A commuter:** how can I spend less time stuck in traffic, and less stressed while I do?

All four agree it is a bad problem. They disagree on why, and the reason decides which solution is best. The book gives an example. If commuter time is the main concern, encouraging people onto buses might not work, because buses tend to be slower than cars, even though it is a good answer to the environmentalist's version.

The [Shoe Swapper game](/games/shoe-swapper/) is the book's version of this exercise on screen.

## Problem reframing: how to write your own

These steps are ours, built on the book's techniques.

1. **Write the problem in its simplest form.** The book's example is "traffic sucks". A blunt version is a fine start.
2. **List your assumptions.** What are you taking for granted about the problem? The book lists six for traffic.
3. **Test each one.** The book asks you to look at an assumption's exact opposite. If that makes a real difference, keep the assumption in. If not, you might want to drop it.
4. **Reword it three ways.** Change who, what or why. Write each as "How can we ...?"
5. **Split it if it is too big.** The book's "splitting the cherry" method writes the problem in its simplest form, then splits it into two smaller problems, then splits each of those, until you have a tree of small problems you can choose from.
6. **Pick one.** Choose the version that points to the most interesting answers, and write it clearly.

## A template, with weak and stronger versions

A simple template is: **How can we [do what] for [whom] so that [outcome]?** The examples below are ours, written for this guide.

| Weak | Stronger |
|---|---|
| Our newsletter is bad. | How can we make our newsletter worth opening for the people who stopped reading it? |
| We need more customers. | How can we reach the people who need our product and do not know it exists? |
| Meetings take too long. | How can we make decisions in the time the meeting is booked for? |
| Sales are down. | How can we find out why our best customers stopped buying? |

Each stronger version names a person or a goal, and none of them contains a solution. If your statement includes the words "by using" or "with a new", you may have started solving before you finished asking.

## Common mistakes

- **Putting the answer in the question.** "How can we build an app for X?" has already decided what the answer is.
- **Stating the symptom.** "Sales are down" describes what you see, not what is wrong.
- **Making it too big.** If you cannot see where to start, split it into smaller pieces.
- **Keeping the first version.** The book's point is that you should work through several versions before you choose.
- **Writing it alone.** Other people see problems you do not. The Different Strokes exercise exists for this reason.

## Try it

1. Pick a real problem and write it as "How can we ...?"
2. Play the [What's Your Problem? game](/games/whats-your-problem/) with it. The game's six question words push you to look at the problem from six directions before you rewrite it.
3. Compare your first version and your rewrite. If they are the same, play again with a different question.

## Questions

**What is the difference between a problem statement and a goal?** A goal says where you want to end up. A problem statement says what is in the way. The book's "How can we ...?" form covers both, because it describes the problem and points toward a better situation.

**How long should a problem statement be?** One or two sentences. The book says to keep it simple and clear.

**Do I need to include data?** If you have it, yes, and give the source. This page does not use statistics, and the examples marked as ours are illustrations, not real cases.

**Is reframing the same as redefining the problem?** Close. Reframing means looking at the same situation in another way, so that the problem you choose to solve changes.
