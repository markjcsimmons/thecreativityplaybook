## Why the question matters

The Answer Is in the Question chapter opens with an idea that sounds simple: the questions we ask shape the answers we get, and posing the right question is an art in itself. Before you spend your creative energy and money finding solutions to a problem, the chapter says, check that it is the right problem and not a bundle of flimsy assumptions.

The book tells a story about Albert Einstein. When he got home from school, his mother did not ask what grades he had got. She asked whether he had asked any good questions.

## A better question changes the answer

The chapter tells two business stories.

**A company.** When Jorma Ollila became the CEO of Nokia in 1992, the company made paper products, rubber goods and cables. He asked what business Nokia should be in. His answer was mobile communications. The book treats that one question as the start of the company's transformation. It is a 2010 snapshot and Nokia has changed since, so read it as a story about the question, not about the company now.

**A band.** The book's board game uses a band as its example. If the band asks "How do we sell more of our music?", it has assumed that selling music is the best way to make money from music. If it asks "How do we make money from our music?", it has not. The answers might include using the recordings to promote live gigs instead of trying to sell them. The two questions differ by a few words and lead to different businesses.

## Open and closed questions

The Idea Spaghetti chapter makes a related point. Asking open-ended questions is a good way to stimulate broad thinking, as children do. The book's example is a hotel where guests are complaining about waiting for the lifts. A closed question, such as how to make the lifts faster, leads to an engineer and new lifts. A wider question, such as how to stop the guests complaining, leads to magazines and mirrors, which is cheaper and works. The page on [divergent vs convergent thinking](/techniques/divergent-vs-convergent-thinking/) tells the full story.

An open question has many possible answers. A closed one has one. When you want ideas, ask the open kind.

## The six question words

The book's game, What's Your Problem?, starts with a problem written as "How can we ...?" You then throw a die and the number gives you a kind of question: 1 is how, 2 is why, 3 is which, 4 is when, 5 is who and 6 is what. You ask that kind of question about your problem, answer it, and at the end rewrite the problem using what you found.

The lists below are ours. They show how each word might open up the same problem, "How can we get more people to come to our events?" The book does not give these lists, so use them as prompts.

- **How.** How are people hearing about us now? How would a first-time visitor describe the evening?
- **Why.** Why do people come? Why do some stop coming? Why do we want more of them?
- **Which.** Which people do we most want to reach? Which events work best, and which do not?
- **When.** When do people decide to come? When do they decide not to?
- **Who.** Who else could invite them? Who is already coming and why?
- **What.** What is stopping people? What would make them tell a friend?

Each question moves you a little away from the first wording. By the end you might rewrite the problem as "How can we make events that people bring a friend to?", which is a different problem and a better one.

## "How might we" and "how can we"

Design teams often use a phrase that starts "How might we ...?" to turn a problem into an open question. It is a popular way to start brainstorms. The book does not use it. Its own wording is "How can we ...?", and the chapter says to keep it simple and clear.

The two do the same job. Both turn a problem into a question that has more than one answer and leaves out the solution. Pick the one your team will use.

### How might we examples

These examples are ours. Each turns a complaint into an open question.

| The complaint | A question to work on |
|---|---|
| Nobody reads our reports. | How might we make our reports something people want to open? |
| Onboarding takes too long. | How might we get a new hire useful in their first week? |
| The office is too noisy. | How might we give people a place to think when they need one? |

## Question storming

Instead of brainstorming answers, some teams brainstorm questions. They write as many questions about a problem as they can and only then choose which to answer. This practice, sometimes called question storming, is not in the book. It follows the same idea: if the question shapes the answer, it is worth making many questions before choosing one.

You can try it with the [Idea Spaghetti game](/games/idea-spaghetti/) by asking it for questions instead of ideas.

## Common mistakes

- **Putting the answer in the question.** "How can we build an app for this?" has already chosen the answer.
- **Asking only closed questions.** "Should we do X?" invites yes or no. "What could we do?" invites options.
- **Stopping at the first version.** The band's first question was not wrong, only narrower than it needed to be.
- **Treating a question as stupid.** The epigraph at the start of the chapter, credited to Anonymous, says the only stupid question is one not asked.
- **Asking alone.** Different people ask different questions. The book's Different Strokes exercise has a mayor, a police chief, an environmentalist and a commuter each look at the same traffic problem.

## Try it

1. Write a real problem as "How can we ...?"
2. Play the [What's Your Problem? game](/games/whats-your-problem/). It gives you six question words and asks you to rewrite your problem at the end.
3. Show both versions to someone and ask which one they would rather work on.

## Questions

**What makes a question good?** A good question is open, so it has many answers. It names a person or a goal. It does not contain the answer.

**Is "how might we" better than "how can we"?** Neither is better. The book uses "How can we ...?", and design teams often use "How might we ...?". Both do the same job.

**How many versions of the problem should I write?** The book's game gives you eight steps. Three or four rewrites is usually enough to see the problem differently.

**What if I cannot think of a question?** Roll the die in the game and ask whatever kind of question it gives you, even if it feels odd. The odd ones are often useful.
