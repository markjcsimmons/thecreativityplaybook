## What the book says a creative culture is

The last chapter of the book, Blast Off!, ends with a summary of what it takes to bring ideas to life. It says a creative culture is one where people are not only rewarded for successes but are allowed, and actively encouraged, to experiment and make mistakes. The chapter says we can make chance ideas more likely with a handful of simple things:

1. keeping notebooks for capturing ideas,
2. holding idea jams, which show a commitment to brave ideas across the organization,
3. embracing play and risk-taking,
4. hiring people who go against the grain, even people who do not seem to fit, and
5. getting the mix of rewards right, in a collaborative environment with a clear, shared vision.

We add a sixth, from Chapter 7: how leaders behave. The sections below go through each, and the sixth comes last.

**A note on the examples.** The stories come from the 2010 edition. The companies in them have changed since, so read each one for the practice it shows, not as a report on how the company works today.

## Practice 1: capture ideas in notebooks

The chapter starts with chance. Penicillin and the microwave oven both came from accidents, and the book says we can make that kind of chance more likely: we come across ideas all the time, often unrelated to what we are working on, and they can connect later. To stop them floating away, carry a notebook. Write down any thoughts or ideas, then look through it regularly to see if they spark something for what you are working on.

The book also uses notebooks to run a group brainstorm over weeks instead of an hour or two:

1. Write the problem clearly on the first page.
2. Ask each person for one idea a day for four weeks.
3. Every few days, give the group some new piece of relevant information or stimulus.
4. At the end of the month, each person writes down the one idea they think is best, with some suggestions for exploring it.
5. They also write down the ideas that seem unrelated to the problem, to build an ideas bank for future projects.

## Practice 2: hold idea jams

An idea jam is, in the book's words, the freestyle jazz of idea creation. People, whether or not they work on the same project, gather once a month to share the ideas they have captured in their notebooks. Put the names in a hat to see who starts. The chosen person picks one idea or thought from the notebook and explains it. Others add their take and any relevant thoughts from their own notebooks.

The book admits a jam with no set problem can look like a waste of time for a busy business, and gives four reasons to disagree. Having and sharing ideas feels good. Jams train and sharpen creative abilities. The ideas may turn out to be relevant, or even lead to a valuable subsidiary business. And businesses that have tried them have had good success. As examples, the chapter tells how IBM held its first World Jam in 2001 as a three-day web-based moderated brainstorm, and a far larger one in 2006, and says that, by IBM's account, new businesses were launched as a result. We have not checked that account.

## Practice 3: embrace play and risk-taking

The chapter says a culture of innovation is more than a cool room with creative toys and a whiteboard. It tells of researchers at London Business School who studied companies that use play to stimulate creativity. They concluded that businesses encourage play in three ways:

- by creating a playful environment,
- by giving employees the freedom, time and resources to make play a part of their work, and
- by giving them the safety to experiment in areas that are not necessarily part of the company's core business.

The book's examples, as of 2010:

- **Pixar.** Employees were entitled to spend four hours a week at Pixar University, taking classes and workshops, and were encouraged not to study anything related to their jobs.
- **W.L. Gore.** The company encouraged employees to play with new ideas to see what came of it. The book tells how an engineer playing with cables on a mountain bike led to the Elixir guitar strings, a business Gore had not been in before.
- **Samsung.** The book is frank that play is hard in a culture where hard work looks like hard work. It says that, at the company's innovation centre near Seoul, the engineers started tidying up and the designers stood and said nothing, and that the centre nonetheless came up with a wine-glass-shaped flatscreen TV.

The book's point is that play is not always easy to introduce.

## Practice 4: hire people who go against the grain

The chapter's summary says to hire people who go against the grain and even seem not to fit. It backs this up in three ways.

**Challengers.** The book cites a 2008 IBM and Economist Intelligence Unit CEO study that said businesses need "visionary challengers", people who question assumptions and suggest radical alternatives. It adds the music-industry deal-maker Danny Socolof on "internal entrepreneurs", who drive change in large companies by taking risks, sometimes without permission.

**Different identities.** The chapter cites a working paper on people with more than one social identity, for example being both Asian and American, or both a woman and an engineer. The researchers found such people showed higher creativity on problems that used the knowledge of their different perspectives. The book draws the conclusion that a workplace where people do not have to hide a part of who they are should help them be more creative.

**Radiators, not drains.** In a sidebar about his own office, Dave Stewart says that if you are in a business that needs creative input, which he says is all businesses, it is best to hire "radiators" rather than "drains", and people he calls Fast Positive People.

### Mix different kinds of people

Tero Ojanperä, then an executive at Nokia, told the authors why mixing is hard:

> For companies it's really a challenge to put together the different types of people, because very often the like-minded people gravitate together. Engineers talk to engineers. Artists will talk to artists. How can we break that mould? How can we force people who think differently together?

*Tero Ojanperä, then an Executive Vice-President at Nokia, in an interview with the authors. From Chapter 12 of the book (2010 edition).*

Nokia's answer was an Ideas Camp that invited really different people with an agenda formed on the spot. The Idea Spaghetti chapter makes the same point about brainstorms: a mix of backgrounds produces a mix of ideas.

## Practice 5: get the mix of rewards right

The chapter asks how important money and other external rewards, such as a job title and recognition, are in motivating creative work. It says studies suggest that when people think they will be rewarded for creativity, they are in fact more creative. A review of studies the book quotes says that the expectation of a reward for creativity leads people to treat the task as one that needs creativity, to become immersed in it and to look for new ways of doing it. If people think a reward depends on not being creative, they will not be.

It is not that simple. The chapter says intrinsic rewards, such as intellectual curiosity or seeing a creative task as a personal challenge, are also big drivers. It cites a survey of research and development employees in the United States that found people working on early-stage research were more productive if they were motivated by the intellectual challenge, and that independence mattered more to them than to people in later stages.

The mood of the workplace counts too. The book cites a study based on workers' diary entries, which found people were more likely to have creative ideas on the days they reported the most positive moods, and that the effect carried into the next day. If people see work in a positive light, with good bosses, a collaborative spirit, a creative vision and rewards for creativity, they are more likely to be creative than where there is infighting and an aversion to new ideas and risk. The chapter's conclusion is that the best business playground depends on the right mix of these things, in a collaborative environment with a shared, clear vision.

## Practice 6: lead for creativity

Chapter 7 looks at the leader of the pack. It cites research that found a leader's negative behaviors had a more extreme effect than positive ones. A bad boss is more bad than a good boss is good, so cutting out destructive behaviors matters at least as much as adding positive ones. It says good leaders do not try to manage creativity, they manage for it, by providing an environment and a culture that lets it flourish. They see themselves as champions of others' ideas, not as the source of ideas that other people carry out. The book adds that the founders of Google tracked ideas that came from them and from others, and found the ideas from elsewhere in the company had a greater success rate. That is a 2010 claim we have not checked.

Leaders also need to decrease the fear of failure, which the book says means celebrating failure as much as success. The director Matthew Warchus does it by getting people on their feet on the first day of rehearsal, so nobody builds up a fear of the moment of starting. The lesson the book draws is not to wait too long before trying out an idea in some form.

## Creative office design

People search for "creative office design" and "creative workspace ideas" as if a room were the answer. The book is more careful. It says a culture of innovation is more than a cool room with toys and a whiteboard, and its creativity quiz says the right environment takes more than how creative the space looks.

The book does say space helps. In a sidebar, Dave Stewart describes a London members' club he helped create, with lots of room to sit around and talk one to one or in groups, and a calendar of events. He wonders why large corporations do not have places like it. He says companies such as Pixar and Google have basketball courts and hang-out places such as cafes on site, and that it helps people exchange ideas freely, as opposed to every conversation being a meeting with people sitting around an oblong table. In his own office, he says there are no real rules or guidelines, staff come and go through the day, and anyone can join in a discussion even when it is not their field.

The Idea Spaghetti chapter says the same about brainstorms: an informal setting, without too much formality or rigidity, helps.

The book's lesson is that a space can make meeting and talking easy. The practices above decide whether people use it.

## Where to start

The book gives practices, not a program. These first steps are ours, for a small team, and none needs a budget.

1. **Give everyone a notebook.** Ask for one idea a day for a week, as a trial.
2. **Hold one idea jam.** A monthly hour, names in a hat, no set problem.
3. **Pick one thing to try that is not your core business.** The London Business School study calls this the safety to experiment.
4. **Invite one person who does not usually come.** The book's rule is a mix of backgrounds.
5. **Celebrate one failure out loud.** Say what the team learned.
6. **Check your own behavior.** The book's research says a leader's negative behaviors weigh more than the good ones.

## Common mistakes

- **Buying the room, skipping the practice.** A cool space with no time to use it changes nothing.
- **Asking for ideas, then judging them.** People notice, and they stop.
- **Copying a company's example.** The book's stories show a practice. Your version needs to fit your people.
- **Rewards that punish risk.** If a reward depends on not being creative, the book says people will not be.
- **A team that is all the same.** The book says mixing is hard and worth doing.
- **One-off events.** An idea jam helps when it is regular.

## Questions

**What is an innovation culture?** The book describes a culture where people are rewarded for successes, and also allowed and encouraged to experiment and make mistakes.

**How do you build an innovation culture?** The book's list is notebooks, idea jams, play and risk-taking, hiring people who go against the grain, and the right mix of rewards in a collaborative setting with a clear vision. Start small with one of them.

**Is money a good motivator for creativity?** The book says studies suggest the expectation of a reward for creativity makes people more creative, and that intrinsic rewards such as challenge and curiosity matter too. It says the mix is the thing.

**Do we need a creative office?** No. The book says a culture is more than a cool room. Space helps people meet, but it does not replace the practices.

**Do these examples still apply?** The companies' details have changed since 2010. The practices are the useful part.
