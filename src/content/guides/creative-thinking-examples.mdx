## What counts as a creative thinking example

Search for creative thinking examples and you will mostly find lists of skills: open-mindedness, curiosity, flexibility. Skills are hard to picture. A story is easier.

The book's own test, from its early chapters, is that an idea has to be both unique and useful. A new idea that does nothing is only odd, and a useful one that everybody has already had is not creative. The stories below meet both tests, and each shows one skill in action.

The examples come from the 2010 edition. Where a story is about a company, read it as a story about the thinking, not a report on the company today.

## Examples of reframing the problem

### 1. A company that asked what business it was in

When Jorma Ollila became the CEO of Nokia in 1992, the company made paper products, rubber goods and cables. He asked what business Nokia should be in, and his answer was mobile communications. The book treats that one question as the start of the company's transformation.

**The skill:** asking about the whole business, not just the next product. **Try it:** write down "What business are we really in?" for your team and answer it three ways. See [asking better questions](/guides/asking-better-questions/).

### 2. A band's two questions

If a band asks "How do we sell more of our music?", it has assumed that selling music is the best way to make money from music. If it asks "How do we make money from our music?", it has not. The answers might include using the recordings to promote live gigs instead.

**The skill:** spotting the assumption hidden in a question. **Try it:** play the [What's Your Problem? game](/games/whats-your-problem/) on a real problem.

### 3. Los Angeles traffic, reworded four ways

The chapter's running example is traffic congestion in Los Angeles. "Reduce congestion" points to roads and transit. "Reduce the need to travel" points to working from home and local services. "Get people to use their cars less" points to fees and incentives. The problem is the same, and the solutions are different.

**The skill:** testing assumptions, such as whether people need cars at all. **Try it:** reword one of your problems three ways. The [problem statement examples](/guides/problem-statement-examples/) page has a template.

## Examples of making and building ideas

### 4. A hotel with slow lifts

Guests are complaining about waiting for the lifts. A manager who thinks convergently hires an engineer to make the lifts faster. A manager who thinks divergently asks how to stop the guests complaining, and ends up giving guests magazines and mirrors to use while they wait, which is cheaper and works.

**The skill:** making many options before choosing one. **Try it:** play [Idea Spaghetti](/games/idea-spaghetti/), then read [divergent vs convergent thinking](/techniques/divergent-vs-convergent-thinking/).

### 5. A random word on a traffic jam

The authors opened a dictionary at random and landed on "donation". They forced it onto the Los Angeles traffic problem and in a couple of minutes had five ideas, including drivers donating to charity each time they drive and employers donating a day a week of staff time to work that does not need travel. Not all of them are good, and the authors say so. They got past the "this word has nothing to do with my problem" barrier.

**The skill:** forcing a connection between unrelated things. **Try it:** the [Random Idea Generator](/tools/random-idea-generator/).

### 6. Seven ideas stacked into one

The book's Chapter 7 technique, building with blocks, starts with small ideas and adds them together. In the Los Angeles example, "make all lanes car pool lanes" plus "build more roads" gives roads for car-poolers. Add public transport and you get charging people who do not car pool and using the money for transport. Add incentives for employers and the stack ends at luxury minibuses with wi-fi and coffee that run in the car pool lane, so staff start work on the way in.

**The skill:** combining ideas so that a group owns the result. **Try it:** see the [brainstorming games](/exercises/brainstorming-games/) page for steps.

## Examples of seeing it differently

### 7. A celebrity's shoes

In the book's Shoe Swapper game, a player landed on the celebrity's shoes while thinking about a restaurant. That produced the idea of celebrity gift bags for customers. The gift bags led to a second idea: make every customer feel like a celebrity, with a fake photographer at the door and signed photos on the wall.

**The skill:** thinking from someone else's point of view, then building on the first idea. **Try it:** the [Shoe Swapper game](/games/shoe-swapper/).

### 8. A musical on a chart

The director Matthew Warchus breaks a stage musical into a chart because, he says, the emotional effect of a story comes from patterns, and only a chart lets you see the patterns. For the show described in the book, each block was a scene, and blocks joined by a color were connected by a thread. Looking at the whole from a distance raised Big Questions, such as what the musical was really about.

**The skill:** making a problem visible. **Try it:** draw your project as boxes on one page and join the ones that belong together. See [creative problem solving](/guides/creative-problem-solving/).

### 9. A sports car broken into five principles

The designers of the Mazda MX-5 started from a design credo, a Japanese phrase that translates roughly as "rider and horse as one". They broke it into five elements, such as compact and light but safe, and an even 50:50 weight split front to rear. The book says the car stayed true to those principles through three generations. A feeling became five things the designers could check.

**The skill:** splitting a large goal into pieces. **Try it:** the fishbone and "splitting the cherry" methods, both in the [creative problem solving](/guides/creative-problem-solving/) guide.

### 10. A juggling picture and a simile

In the Brain Flash game, you throw dice, find a picture and complete "It is like ... because ...". In the book's example, a picture of juggling skittles became a simile about acrobats and speed. The same picture could just as well have become a simile about coordination, leading to different ideas.

Outside the book, comparing two things like this is called analogical thinking, or thinking by analogy. The book calls it a simile or a metaphor. **The skill:** letting a comparison suggest ideas. **Try it:** the [Brain Flash game](/games/brain-flash/).

## Examples of finding ideas that are already out there

### 11. An umbrella with a bug list

The Scavengers chapter tells how the steel-ribbed umbrella has flaws: it blows inside out, it breaks and it can poke someone in the eye. A Dutch company used those flaws as a list of things to fix and redesigned the umbrella with an asymmetrical shape and eye protectors for bystanders. The book says the designers told the authors there was no eureka moment. First came a solid analysis of everything that goes wrong, then solutions for each.

**The skill:** treating irritations as a list of opportunities. **Try it:** see [invention ideas](/guides/invention-ideas/) for the bug-list method.

### 12. Mold in a dish, and a chocolate bar in a pocket

The book says penicillin and the microwave oven both started by chance. Alexander Fleming noticed that mold on a dish had killed the bacteria around it. Percy Spencer, working near a machine that produces microwave signals with a chocolate bar in his pocket, found it had melted. Both noticed something odd and did not ignore it.

The book's advice is to be ready to catch such ideas: carry a notebook, write down whatever occurs to you and look back over it regularly. **The skill:** noticing what others overlook. **Try it:** keep a notebook for a week. The site's [Idea Notebook](/notebook/) works for this.

## More stories worth knowing

- **A red paperclip.** Kyle MacDonald traded a single paperclip, in 14 swaps over a year, for a house in Saskatchewan. It shows how a small, simple idea that can be said in a sentence gets retold. See [storytelling in business](/guides/storytelling-in-business/).
- **An engineer on a bike.** The book tells how a W.L. Gore engineer playing with cables on a mountain bike ended up leading the company into guitar strings. It shows play and freedom to experiment, told as 2010 history. See [innovation culture](/guides/innovation-culture/).
- **A distracted mind.** In a study the book describes, students who spent three minutes on a dull task before a creative one gave more original answers than students who did not. See [whole brain thinking](/guides/whole-brain-thinking/) and the [Wheel of Distraction](/games/wheel-of-distraction/).

## How to learn from an example

These steps are ours.

1. **Name the move.** In one sentence, what did the thinker do differently? Reworded the problem? Added a random word? Took another person's view?
2. **Find the hidden assumption.** What did everybody else take for granted?
3. **Try the move on your own problem** for five minutes.
4. **Note what changed.** If the ideas are better or at least different, keep the move.

## Common mistakes

- **Copying the answer.** The Nokia, band and umbrella stories are about how the thinking went, not about what to do in your business.
- **Treating them as proof.** They are the book's stories, mostly told in 2010. They illustrate; they do not measure.
- **Skipping the unglamorous part.** The umbrella designers said there was no eureka moment. Solid analysis came first.
- **Collecting examples and not trying them.** Each story has a way to try it.

## Questions

**What is an example of creative thinking?** A band that asks how to make money from its music instead of how to sell its music is one. It questions the assumption inside its first question and finds other answers.

**What is an example of creative thinking at work?** The hotel's magazines and mirrors, the umbrella's bug list and Nokia's question are all workplace examples from the book.

**Is creative thinking a talent?** The book says some people are more creative than others, but that everyone can think creatively and that certain activities bring the ability out.

**Do I need to be an artist?** No. Few of these stories involve art. They involve questions, lists, charts and random words.
