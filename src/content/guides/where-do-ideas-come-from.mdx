## The book's answer: ideas are lying around

Search for where ideas come from and you will find essays on inspiration, memory and the unconscious. The book is more practical. Its Scavengers chapter says great ideas are out there waiting to be found, and you have to be hunting for them.

That turns a mystery into a list of places to look. Here are six, in the order the book teaches them.

## 1. Things that bug you

The first place is irritation. The Scavengers chapter says to list what you do not like about a product, a service or a whole industry. If you can get rid of a bug, it could be the basis for a wonderful innovation.

The authors' own bug list includes junk mail, shrink-wrapped packaging, parking and tasteless fruit. Everything on it is a question that someone could answer.

**Try it:** write a list of 30 things that annoy you. The full method is in [invention ideas](/guides/invention-ideas/). The [Scavengers game](/games/scavengers/) trains the noticing habit.

## 2. Things you can take apart

The second place is any product that already exists. Break it into its attributes and list the options for each. A restaurant has a type of food, a price, a style and a way the food is delivered, and each has many possibilities. Then recombine them. Expensive Italian food delivered to businesses is one combination, and it may or may not work.

**Try it:** the [Attribute Listing tool](/tools/attribute-listing/) has ready-made charts.

## 3. The future

The third place is a long way ahead. The book calls it ball-gazing. It says that if we look only five years ahead we tend to assume the world will look much like it does now, and so we underestimate big shifts. Looking at least ten years ahead makes it easier to drop short-term trends.

The authors then throw in disruptions that may have happened along the way, some silly on purpose. For your own work, they suggest imagining the big breaks in your industry, not the small steps that follow logically from what is already happening. Thinking through odd scenarios can help you find better ways to do or make something.

**Try it:** write down three huge changes that could hit your industry in ten years. For each, ask what a customer would need then.<IfLive path="/guides/moonshot-thinking/"> The [moonshot thinking](/guides/moonshot-thinking/) guide covers the habit of thinking big.</IfLive>

## 4. Other people's eyes

The fourth place is someone else's view. The Shoe Swapper chapter says we are held back by what we know, and that a child, a cab passenger or a person from an unrelated profession will see the problem differently.

Dave Stewart's story in the chapter makes the point in reverse. A London cab driver told him he was fed up with driving and that it was all he knew. Dave pointed out how much he knew about the city and the job, and suggested turning it into a board game. Two years later the driver came back with the game on sale. Sometimes, Dave says, you do not know what you know. A fresh pair of eyes can see what else your own knowledge could be used for.

**Try it:** the [Shoe Swapper game](/games/shoe-swapper/).<IfLive path="/guides/perspective-taking/"> The [perspective taking](/guides/perspective-taking/) guide has more.</IfLive>

## 5. Things that have nothing to do with it

The fifth place is the unrelated. The Connected chapter says creativity is about making connections between things that seem unrelated, and that you can add chance on purpose. The authors opened a dictionary at random, landed on "donation" and forced it onto the problem of Los Angeles traffic. In a couple of minutes they had five ideas.

The chapter's "Joker" prompts do the same with everyday sources: the fridge, five television channels, a newspaper, a magazine, a library of books you would not normally read. You ask how each connects to your problem.

**Try it:** the [random word technique](/techniques/random-word-technique/) and the [Random Idea Generator](/tools/random-idea-generator/).

## 6. Moments you did not plan

The sixth place is chance, and the book says you have to be ready for it. Penicillin and the microwave oven both began by chance. Alexander Fleming noticed that mold on a dish had killed the bacteria around it. Percy Spencer found that a chocolate bar in his pocket had melted while he stood near a machine that produced microwave signals. Both noticed something odd and did not ignore it.

The chapter's advice is to carry a notebook, write down any thought or idea, and look back through it regularly to see whether it sparks something. Ideas often seem unrelated to the project you are on, and they connect later. For a group, it suggests an idea a day for a month, or a monthly idea jam where people share what is in their notebooks.

The Left Brain chapter adds that stepping away helps. In a study the book describes, people who spent three minutes on a dull task before a creative one gave more original answers. The book's explanation is that the mind keeps working while you are doing something else. That is why ideas turn up in the shower or on a walk. We have not checked the study against the original paper.

**Try it:** keep an [Idea Notebook](/notebook/) for a week, and spin the [Wheel of Distraction](/games/wheel-of-distraction/) when you are stuck.

## A week of hunting

The book does not give a schedule. This one is ours, and takes about 20 minutes a day.

| Day | Where to look | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Monday | Bugs | Write a bug list. Circle three. |
| Tuesday | Take it apart | Attribute-list one product on your list. |
| Wednesday | The future | Ten-year look at your industry, three disruptions. |
| Thursday | Other eyes | Explain your problem to someone outside your field and write down what they ask. |
| Friday | Unrelated things | Two random words. Two minutes each. |
| All week | Chance | Carry a notebook. On Friday, read it back. |

## Common mistakes

- **Waiting for inspiration.** The book's premise is that you hunt. Set a time.
- **Only looking inside your field.** Several of these places are outside it, on purpose.
- **Not writing things down.** Ideas arrive when you are not working, and they go as quickly.
- **Judging while you collect.** Collect first. The [divergent vs convergent thinking](/techniques/divergent-vs-convergent-thinking/) page explains why.
- **Stopping at one idea.** The book's case is that making many ideas raises the odds that one is good.

## Questions

**Where do ideas come from?** According to the book, from noticing: irritations, existing products, the future, other people's eyes, unrelated things and chance. The book treats them as places to hunt, not as a gift.

**Where do good ideas come from?** The book says that the more ideas you make, the more likely it is that one is good. It keeps making ideas and judging them separate.

**Do ideas come from the unconscious?** The book reports a study in which a short distraction helped people give more original answers. It does not claim to know how the mind does it.

**How do I come up with new ideas on demand?** Use the hunts above, or pick a technique from the [ideation techniques](/techniques/) list. The book's games are set up so you can start in five minutes.
