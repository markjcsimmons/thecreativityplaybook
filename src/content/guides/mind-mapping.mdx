## What a mind map is

A mind map puts one topic in the middle and spreads it out as branches. Each main branch is a theme, and smaller branches carry the details. The shape is the point: you can see at a glance where the topic is full and where it is empty.

**The method is not from the book.** Most sources credit the author Tony Buzan with popularizing the term in the 1970s. Branching diagrams are older than that, and sources disagree about how much he invented, so treat that as the usual account.

## What the book does with them

The book shows mind maps without teaching them. In Chapter 6, Dave Stewart includes one from a series made at a session hosted by the business strategist John Kao and the mind-map artist Lesley Evans, with everyone working on five-foot pieces of paper. The book says it is one of about ten that came out of that session. In Chapter 2 there is another, for the vision behind one of Dave's own ideas, shown as one slide from a series made while the idea was worked on. Both were made with other people, and neither comes with rules.

What the book does teach in the same chapter is the reason to draw at all. When people rely on words alone, it says, it is hard to restructure a problem, and a picture lets you see it a different way. A mind map is one of the simplest pictures to draw. Chapter 6's own methods are sketching, photos, a group collage and comparisons, which the [Brain Flash chapter](/chapters/brain-flash/) covers.

## Four mind map examples

Each example is written as a list of branches, because a list is the easiest thing to copy. Type or paste one into the mind map maker on the template page and it draws the map. The first uses the book's own problem, and the other three are made up for this page.

The book's running problem. The seven main branches are the seven smaller problems that Chapter 6 splits it into for the collage exercise. The ideas under them are ours.

### 2. An event: a team away day

All of this is ours.

- **Goal**
  - One problem the team wants help with
  - Something to take back to work
- **People**
  - Who has to be there
  - Who should be invited who would not normally be
- **Place**
  - Somewhere that is not the office
  - Room for small groups
- **Agenda**
  - Morning: make ideas
  - Afternoon: choose and plan
- **Food and breaks**
  - Lunch away from the tables
- **Afterwards**
  - Who does what by when

### 3. A talk: a ten-minute pitch

All of this is ours.

- **Opening**
  - A story, not a slide
- **The problem**
  - Who has it
  - What it costs them
- **The idea**
  - One sentence
  - What it looks like
- **Why now**
- **What I need**
  - A yes, a name or a date
- **Questions I expect**
  - The hardest one first

### 4. Notes: a chapter or an article

All of this is ours.

- **Main point**
  - One sentence in my own words
- **Evidence**
  - The three things that most support it
- **Examples**
  - One I can retell
- **What I disagree with**
- **What I will do differently**

## How to start one

These steps are ours.

1. **Write the topic in the middle, in a few words.**
2. **Add five to eight main branches.** If you cannot think of any, use the six question words: who, what, why, when, how and which. They are the six that the book's What's Your Problem? game uses to open up a problem.
3. **Add ideas to each branch in one or two words.** Keep each label short enough to scan.
4. **Keep going until a branch runs dry, then go back to the empty ones.** An empty branch is a question.
5. **Draw lines between branches that belong together.** On paper, use a different color.
6. **Stop and choose.** A map is a picture of the topic, not a decision. Choose the branches to work on and sort the ideas, for example with the [Idea Scorer](/tools/idea-scorer/).

## When a mind map is the wrong tool

- **You already know the order.** A plan with steps is a list.
- **You need to compare two or three options in detail.** A table does that better.
- **Your map has one long chain.** That is a list drawn sideways.
- **You are working alone and stuck for ideas.** A prompt, such as a random word or a game's dice, may start you faster than a blank center. The book's method for this is the [random word technique](/techniques/random-word-technique/).

## Common mistakes

- **Using sentences.** Long labels turn the map into a page of text.
- **Starting with too few branches.** Two branches make a list. Aim for at least five.
- **Judging as you draw.** The book keeps making ideas and choosing between them apart.
- **Stopping at the first level.** The ideas are on the smaller branches.
- **Treating the map as the finished work.** It is a way to see a topic, and you still have to decide what to do.

## Questions

**What is a mind map?** A diagram with one topic in the middle and branches of related ideas spreading out from it.

**What are some mind map examples?** This page has four: a problem (traffic in Los Angeles), an event (a team away day), a talk (a ten-minute pitch) and a set of notes. Each is written as a branch list.

**Is mind mapping in the book?** The book shows two mind maps, in Chapter 6 and Chapter 2, but does not teach how to make one. The steps on this page are ours.

**How is a mind map different from a brainstorm?** A brainstorm produces a list of ideas. A mind map groups ideas by theme as you go, so you can see where there are gaps. You can brainstorm first and map afterwards, or map to brainstorm.

**How many branches should a mind map have?** Five to eight main branches is workable. More than that, and a second map is usually clearer.

**Do I need special software?** No. Paper and pens work, and so does the free maker on the template page.
