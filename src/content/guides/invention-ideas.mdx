## Where invention ideas come from

The Scavengers chapter of the book says great ideas are lying around, and you have to be hunting for them. It gives two ways to hunt for new product ideas.

1. **Start with a bug.** A bug is anything you dislike about a product, a service or a whole industry, or anything that irritates you. The chapter says that if you can get rid of one, it could be the basis for a wonderful innovation.
2. **Take a product apart.** Break an existing product into its attributes, list the options for each, and recombine them. The chapter calls this attribute listing.

The steps below are ours, built on the book's two ideas. Both start from something that already exists. You do not need a brand-new category. The book's point is that not accepting the way things are now is a great starting point for innovation.

## Method 1: make a bug list

### How to do it

1. **Set a timer for ten minutes.** Write down every irritation you can think of, small or large. Do not judge them yet.
2. **Include the ones you have stopped noticing.** The things we put up with every day are the easiest to miss.
3. **Circle three you feel strongly about.** Strong feelings point to a real problem for real people.
4. **Ask who else is annoyed.** If only you mind, it is a hobby. If lots of people do, it is a possible product.
5. **Write one way to fix each.** A rough sentence is enough.

### The book's bug list

The authors give their own list and say you are welcome to steal any of it. It includes junk mail, bottled water, piped muzak, dog poop on the pavement, not enough time in the day, air travel, tasteless fruit, useless plastic toys, customer service, parking, "special offers", commercial radio, airport security and shrink-wrapped packaging. They also add that they could go on.

Use a list like that as a prompt, not an answer. Each item is a question. What would a better version of junk mail look like? Why is fruit tasteless, and who would pay for fruit that was not? Each one has a customer who would welcome a fix.

## A worked bug: airplane seats

The chapter looks at airline seats in coach class. The people who design them put safety ahead of comfort, but the authors cite a crash-test expert who says there is no reason a safe seat cannot be a comfortable one. The seat makers blame the airlines for packing the rows too tight. The airlines blame passengers, saying more room means fewer seats and less money. That is a lot of people blaming each other, and a bug that is still there.

It is also a bug with alternatives. The book describes a staggered seat row whose maker says it gives a shoulder width as wide as business class, designs that use the space above passengers, and seats that face each other. The lesson is not that those designs are the answer. It is that a bug with a lot of people blaming each other is a bug with room for an inventor.

## Method 2: attribute listing

The Scavengers chapter breaks a pen down into a look, a feel, a material, a type of ink and a way of releasing the ink. It does the same for a restaurant: the type of food, the price, the style, and how the food is delivered. Each of those has many possible options. A restaurant's food could be Italian, Chinese, Indian or Mexican. The style could be casual, formal, family-oriented or trendy. The food could even be delivered by waiters on skateboards.

Then you try combinations. Expensive Italian food delivered to businesses is one. Mid-priced French food in a leisurely, family-friendly restaurant is another. Some combinations will make a great new experience, and some will be a recipe for disaster. You find out by looking at them.

You can try it with the [Attribute Listing tool](/tools/attribute-listing/), which has ready-made charts and lets you build your own. For a quick starting prompt, the [Product Idea Generator](/tools/invention-idea-generator/) gives you an everyday object, a verb and a user.

### How to do it

1. **Pick a product you know well.** A bicycle, a chair, a phone case.
2. **List three to five attributes.** Choose ones that change independently, such as material, size, price and how it is sold.
3. **List the ordinary options and the odd ones.** The odd ones are where surprises come from.
4. **Change one attribute at a time.** That way you can see which change made the difference.
5. **Ask who would want it.** An invention without a user is a curiosity.

## Examples from the book

### An umbrella, fixed twice

The chapter's best-known example is the umbrella. The steel-ribbed umbrella was invented by Samuel Fox in 1852, although umbrellas in one form or another have been around for thousands of years. It keeps the rain off and folds away, but it has flaws. It is prone to blow inside out or to break, and it can poke someone in the eye.

Those flaws were a bug list, and a Dutch company used them to redesign the umbrella with an asymmetrical shape that works with the wind rather than against it, plus protectors for bystanders' eyes. The book says it went on sale in 2007 for $67 and sold 10,000 units in its first nine days. The book cites ABC News for that figure, and we have not checked it independently. The designers told the authors there was never a eureka moment. First there was a solid analysis of all the problems people have with umbrellas, then solutions for each.

### A recycling genius

The book also tells of one author's stepfather, Julien, in the first chapter. He lived on what he found each day. The book says:

> Julien would only eat, read or play with what he found each day and he was a recycling genius.

Julien struggled home with old chair legs, coat hangers and door handles, and turned them into inventions, some of which the author says would have sold like hotcakes if they had gone into production. He solved his own problems with what was around him. That is a useful way to hunt for inventions: start with a problem you have and the materials you already have.

### A digital mousetrap

The chapter notes that even the humble mousetrap keeps inspiring new designs and patent applications. In June 2006 the pest-control company Rentokil launched a digital trap that tells operatives when a rodent has been caught and in which particular trap. The old saying goes, "Build a better mousetrap, and the world will beat a path to your door." The authors add: "The world excluding the mice, that is."

## New product ideas: how to tell if one is worth pursuing

A bug and a clever fix are only a start. Before you spend time or money, ask these questions. They are ours, not the book's.

- **Does it already exist?** Search for it. Many ideas that feel new are already on sale.
- **Who would buy it, and why?** Name one real person who would. Better, ask them.
- **Can you make a rough version cheaply?** A scrap prototype teaches more than a plan.
- **Do you need to protect it?** If you think you do, talk to a patent attorney or your national patent office before you share the details widely. This guide is not legal advice.

## Common mistakes

- **Starting with the solution.** Cool gadgets with no problem behind them are hard to sell. Start with the bug.
- **Fixing your own annoyance only.** Check that other people share it.
- **Changing everything at once.** In attribute listing, changing one attribute at a time shows you what worked.
- **Skipping the boring problems.** Junk mail and packaging are on the authors' bug list because they affect everyone.
- **Never looking at what exists.** The mousetrap shows that even a very old product still has room to improve.

## Try it today

1. Set a timer for ten minutes and write a bug list. Aim for 30 items.
2. Circle three and write one way to fix each.
3. Pick the one you like best and open the [Attribute Listing tool](/tools/attribute-listing/) to take the related product apart.
4. Run the [Product Idea Generator](/tools/invention-idea-generator/) a few times and see whether any prompt connects to your bug.

## Questions

**Are these invention ideas I can use?** The page shows how to find them, not a list of vetted products. The book's bugs are prompts. Any idea you pick still needs checking.

**Do I need to be an engineer?** No. The book's examples include a person who redesigned an umbrella from a list of problems and a man who made inventions out of things he found on the street.

**Does a better version of an old product count as an invention idea?** Here it does. The words overlap, and the book's umbrella and mousetrap examples are both improvements on very old products.

**Where does attribute listing come from?** The Scavengers chapter describes it. Outside the book it is often called morphological analysis.
