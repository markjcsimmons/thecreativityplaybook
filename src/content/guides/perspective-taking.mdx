## Two meanings of perspective taking

In psychology, perspective taking usually means working out what another person thinks or feels. It is studied as part of empathy, and most pages on the subject are about getting along with people.

This page uses the book's meaning. The Shoe Swapper chapter is about getting ideas, not understanding people. When you are close to a problem you stop seeing it. Looking at it as someone else would, a child or a passenger in a cab, gives you ideas that are not held back by what you already know. That is the sense used here.

## Why expertise gets in the way

The chapter does not say experts are a bad thing. Expertise helps you define the creative challenge, filter your ideas and carry out the ones with the best chance. But it can also get in the way of making ideas, because knowing too much can inhibit the creative process.

The authors put it like this: we know the rules, we know what has been done before and what works, so we think we know what is not worth trying. And often we are wrong. The book quotes Andrew Grove, a co-founder of Intel, who believed the best way to tackle a problem is to set aside everything you already know.

### The tappers and the listeners

The book gives a study to show how knowledge skews judgment. People in an experiment were randomly made either tappers or listeners. Tappers tapped out a well-known song, such as Happy Birthday, with their knuckles on a table, and listeners tried to name it. The tappers knew the tune, so they were sure it was obvious. They expected listeners to name it far more often than listeners did.

The book says the result was described in a journal as "the curse of knowledge": once you are an expert in a field, it is hard to imagine not knowing what you know. We have not checked the study against the original, and we leave out its figures.

## Who to borrow fresh eyes from

The book's phrase for the answer is "inexpert witnesses". In a court, an expert witness is brought in because of what they know. The authors suggest the opposite for creative work: bring in people who are unencumbered by too much experience or too many rules, and who do not know what has been tried and what has not.

They name several kinds of person.

- **People from other parts of your organization,** especially from a different discipline, with little or no familiarity with the problem.
- **People further removed from the business.** The book lists roofers, writers, musicians, sushi chefs and jockeys, as people who can look at the problem through fresh pairs of eyes.
- **Children.** The authors call a child's perspective naive by definition, because it has not yet been tainted by the rules of business and the marketplace. They suggest finding a very simple way to explain your challenge and asking some kids for ideas. Or ask a teacher to turn it into a class project, such as what a class of five-year-olds suggests about too much traffic, and use what they come up with as a metaphor for looking at your problem in a new way. They add that most children do not charge big hourly fees.

## "You don't know what you know"

Dave Stewart tells a story in this chapter about a London cab driver. The driver told him that he no longer enjoyed the job. He felt he was stuck, because driving a cab was all he knew how to do. Dave pointed out how much he did know: the streets, the ups and downs of the job, how to judge people's temperament, how to stay safe. He suggested turning all of that into a board game, giving it a name, registering it and meeting a game company.

About two years later the driver turned up at his door with the finished game, made by a game company, and he was a royalty-earning inventor. Dave's moral is that sometimes "you don't know what you know". The cab driver had put his knowledge in a very small box. It took someone outside the box to see what else it could be used for.

It works the other way too. If you are the expert, an outsider can tell you what your knowledge is worth.

## The Shoe Swapper exercise

The book turns this into a game you play alone. You pick a problem, then in each of three rounds you spend five minutes writing down ideas from the point of view of a different person, whose shoes you land on when you throw the dice. You can play it online as the [Shoe Swapper game](/games/shoe-swapper/).

The book's example is a restaurant that wants more customers. One round landed on a celebrity's shoes. From there came an idea of gift bags for customers, and then a second idea built on the first: make every customer feel like a celebrity, with a fake photographer at the door and signed photos on the wall.

The chapter's lesson is that you can use the same thing without the game: pick a point of view you are not in, and write down what you would do if you were that person.

## Five different ways of looking

The chapter has a second device. It asks you to picture a gang of children who go on adventures together. They argue, and they always work together against any outside enemy. Each child approaches a problem differently:

- **Naoki** looks for solutions that take into account the feelings of as many people as possible.
- **Shiori** thinks very broadly about the problem and the other areas it might be linked to.
- **Ryota** wants a solution quickly and does not ponder whether it is the right one.
- **Bunko** takes the opposite approach and wants to explore every avenue before reaching a conclusion.
- **Arata** is determined to come up with something nobody has tried, and cares more about the unknown than about the results.

Each approach has merit, the authors say, and imagining how each would handle your problem helps you switch into different ways of solving it. When you have some ideas, ask which child would agree with each one, what arguments would follow and how you could reconcile them. Is there an idea radical enough for Arata that does not upset Naoki?

These are archetypes of thinking style, not real people, which makes them easy to use with a team.

## A second version: roles with reasons

The Chapter 4 team version is called Different Strokes. In the Los Angeles traffic example, a mayor, a police chief, an environmentalist and a commuter all agree traffic is a problem. They disagree about why. The mayor worries about lost business, the police chief about accidents, the environmentalist about exhaust fumes and the commuter about wasted time and stress.

The reason decides which solution is best. If the time spent traveling is the key issue, buses that make many stops may not help, even if they are good for the environment. The authors suggest giving each member of a small team a role, a name and even a prop. Seeing which role cares about what is also a way to define the problem before you start solving it.

## How to do it

These steps are ours, built on the book's examples.

1. **Write the problem as a question.** "How can we ...?" works. See [asking better questions](/guides/asking-better-questions/).
2. **List four or five viewpoints.** Mix them: someone who knows nothing about your field, someone who cares about a different outcome, someone with a different thinking style.
3. **Give each viewpoint five minutes.** Write ideas as that person, in first person if it helps. Aim for quantity, not quality.
4. **Do not stop at "they would not care".** The book's skill is getting past the barrier of thinking there is no link.
5. **Choose the ideas that surprised you.** Then ask which viewpoint would reject each one, and whether it can be changed to suit them.
6. **Take it to a real person.** An imagined view only gets you so far. If one idea matters, describe the problem in a sentence to someone outside your field and listen to what they ask.

### A starter list of viewpoints

These are ours, and they are roles, not individuals.

| Viewpoint | What it makes you ask |
|---|---|
| Someone on their first day | What is confusing that I no longer notice? |
| Someone who has never heard of your field | What would I have to explain in one sentence? |
| A child | What is the simplest version of this? |
| Someone who works with their hands | What would I build or fix first? |
| Your harshest critic | What would they say is wrong with this? |
| Someone with no money | What would I do if I could not buy a solution? |
| Someone with all the money | What would I do if cost did not matter? |

## Common mistakes

- **Treating it as a way to know what someone thinks.** The book's version is imagined viewpoints for making ideas. If you want to know what a real customer thinks, ask them. Guessing is not the same.
- **Picking viewpoints too close to your own.** Another manager in your department sees what you see.
- **Judging while you write.** The ideas from the odd viewpoints are meant to be rough.
- **Listening to the expert only.** The book's whole point is that the person who knows least may ask the best question.
- **Stopping at the first idea.** The celebrity gift bag led to a second, better idea. Keep building on the first.

## Questions

**What is perspective taking?** In psychology, it is working out how someone else sees things. In the book, it is looking at a problem through someone else's eyes in order to make ideas. This page is about the second.

**Is perspective taking the same as empathy?** Not in the book. Empathy is about understanding a person. The book's version is a tool for making ideas, and it does not claim to teach you what anyone feels.

**Why ask a child?** The book says a child's view is naive and has not been shaped by the rules of business. That is the quality it wants.

**Can I do it alone?** Yes. The Shoe Swapper game is played alone. Teams can use Different Strokes, with roles and names.

**Does it work for personal problems?** The book's examples are about business and city problems. The method of borrowing a viewpoint is general, but the book does not test it on personal ones.
