## What creativity in business means

Creativity in business is the ability to come up with ideas and bring them to life in the marketplace. The book's definition has two halves. An idea has to be new, and it has to be useful. A clever idea that goes nowhere is not much use, and a useful idea that everyone has already had is not creative.

The Introduction to the original edition describes creativity as a way to get an advantage over competitors, and says it is not really a secret. In surveys, the book says, executives said creativity would drive their businesses. Yet it adds that creativity is all too often not part of the culture. We leave out the survey figures, which are from 2008 and have not been rechecked.

## The case for it

### Doing more of the same stops working

Tim Brown, the head of the design firm IDEO when the book was written, gave the authors the clearest version. Once you are past a certain level of quality and efficiency, he said, there is very little competitive advantage in doing more of what you do, because everybody else learns just as fast. The alternative is new choices that did not exist before.

Tero Ojanperä, then a senior executive at Nokia, described two kinds of progress. Most companies drive linear innovation, doing what they have always done a little better. That helps up to a point, then it produces little, and someone arrives with a non-linear idea and bypasses you. His point is that companies typically fail when they continue on the old path.

### Most businesses are built for efficiency

The Introduction's second argument is that businesses are not built for creativity. They tend to be efficient machines, with processes, systems and rules that leave little room for the unstructured thinking ideas need.

The book's example is 3M, which had been famed for its creativity and, as the book tells it, had begun to focus on efficiency at the expense of it. Its then CEO said that invention is a disorderly process. Art Fry, who invented the Post-it note, questioned whether his idea would see the light of day in a company that preferred efficiency to experiment. This is the 2010 edition's account, and the company has changed since, so read it as a story about the tension.

### The new edition says it again

The new edition's Introduction, which is on this site as [About The Creativity Playbook](/introduction/), makes the same case for today. When more and more of work is optimized and data-driven, it says, plain efficiency will not be enough, and creativity is what makes people feel seen and understood. It also takes up how A.I. changes the picture, and argues that creativity is a conversation between different voices, not a contest.

## Creativity is not a department

The book warns of a common mistake. It is tempting to cordon creativity off in certain departments, send it to off-site meetings, or outsource it to agencies and consultants. The authors call that a shame, because everybody has the ability to be creative.

Tim Brown told the authors that the myth is you have to wear black turtlenecks and designer spectacles to be creative. Some people are born with more talent, he said, but everybody can use creativity techniques to be more productive and have better ideas than they otherwise would. Jack Welch, the former CEO of General Electric, urged companies to use the brains of every worker, to make sure the best idea wins and to reward and celebrate new ideas so others want to contribute.

The Introduction adds that we are all born with great creative skills, and that these are sidelined or smothered by the rigidity of schools and businesses. It also cites a researcher's view, as the book reports it, that adults deprived of play tend to be rigid and closed to new options, and that playfulness helps us adapt. That is the case behind the earlier edition's title, The Business Playground.

## Creative leadership: what the book asks of a leader

Many people who search for creative leadership are looking for research on leaders and teams. The book does not cover that research, and neither does this page. What the book does say can be put in a short list. The summary is ours.

- **Do not cordon it off.** Make creativity part of everyday business, not an annual event.
- **Reward ideas, not only results.** Welch's advice, as the book gives it, is to make sure the best idea wins and to celebrate new ones.
- **Make room for play.** The Introduction argues that play is a vital ingredient and often at odds with the serious business world.
- **Keep making and judging separate.** The book's whole method is to make many ideas first and choose later. See [divergent vs convergent thinking](/techniques/divergent-vs-convergent-thinking/).
- **Mix different kinds of people.** The last chapter says a mix of perspectives and skills keeps things fresh.
- **Capture ideas.** The last chapter recommends notebooks and a monthly idea jam.

The last two are covered in more detail in the [innovation culture](/guides/innovation-culture/) guide, which goes through the book's practices one by one.

## Where to start

These steps are ours, for a manager or a small team. Start small and run one real problem through the book's method.

1. **Pick a real problem.** Something the team already worries about. Write it as a question. The [problem statement examples](/guides/problem-statement-examples/) guide helps.
2. **Run a 30-minute session.** Play [Idea Spaghetti](/games/idea-spaghetti/) or follow the [ideation workshop](/exercises/team-workshop-activities/) agenda, with everyone writing ideas alone first.
3. **Choose.** Use [Kill the Idea](/games/kill-the-idea/) to cut the pile.
4. **Make the winner tangible.** The [Blast Off game](/games/blast-off/) gives it a name, a news story and a crew.
5. **Share what you learned.** Write a paragraph for the team on what you tried. That is the beginning of a habit.
6. **Do it again next month.** The book's point is that a habit beats an event.

## Common mistakes

- **Treating it as a one-off.** An away-day does not make a culture.
- **Asking for ideas and shooting the odd ones down.** The book says to start by naming something good about an idea, so the person is willing to share next time.
- **Measuring only efficiency.** The 3M story is about what that can cost.
- **Leaving it to the "creative" people.** The book's argument is that it is for everyone.
- **Expecting a result in the first hour.** The book says making many ideas is how you find the good one.

## Questions

**Why is creativity important in business?** The book's answer is that it is the competitive edge. When everyone has the same efficiency and quality, new ideas are what set you apart.

**What is the difference between creativity and innovation?** The book defines creativity as coming up with ideas and bringing them to life in the marketplace, so it does not draw a hard line between the two.

**What is creative leadership?** In the book, it is a leader who makes room for ideas, rewards them and keeps making and judging separate. The research literature on creative leadership is outside the book.

**Can anyone be creative at work?** The book says yes. Some people are more gifted, but everybody can use techniques to have better ideas.

**Does creativity matter more now that there is A.I.?** The new edition's Introduction argues that it does. It is on the [About page](/introduction/).
