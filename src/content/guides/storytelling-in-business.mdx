## What this page covers

Plenty of guides teach corporate storytelling: how to open a presentation, how to pick a case study, how to end on a call to action. This one is narrower. It uses the Far Out chapter of the book, which says that thinking big can trigger interesting ideas, and that a good story makes people want to come along.

The chapter's tagline is "Think BIG and don't let the truth get in the way of a good story." It is a joke, and we read it as one. In business, a story you cannot back up costs you trust. Keep the facts true. The rest of the chapter is about making the facts vivid.

## Business storytelling examples

### A paperclip that became a house

Kyle MacDonald, a Canadian, set himself a challenge: see what he could get by trading up from a single red paperclip. He started on 12 July 2005 by advertising the paperclip online, saying he wanted something bigger or better for it. The first offer was a fish-shaped pen. After 14 trades, exactly one year later, he had a house in Kipling, Saskatchewan.

The chain of trades makes the book's point. Nobody retells "a man traded some objects". They retell the red paperclip. The idea was small, simple and easy to explain in a sentence, and every step of it was a new reason to pass it on. The town of Kipling, population 1,140, saw that the story could bring it attention, and offered him a house in exchange for a movie role he had earned along the way.

MacDonald told the authors what the project taught him:

> If you don't take the first step, you'll never go anywhere.

*Kyle MacDonald, creator of the One Red Paperclip project, in an interview with the authors. From Chapter 8 of the book.*

### A musical, charted

In the Answer Is in the Question chapter, the director Matthew Warchus explains why he charts a story. His job, he says, is storytelling, and a script is read in a straight line, but the emotional effect of a story comes from patterns: the mirrors and echoes that recur, such as a park bench or a piece of music that keeps coming back.

> The emotional effect of the story is achieved by patterns, and so only by creating a chart can you start to look at what the patterns are in the story, or what patterns you want to emphasise, or what patterns you want to add to the story.

*Matthew Warchus, director, in an interview with the authors. From Chapter 4 of the book.*

For business, the lesson is that a good story has a shape you can see. A story that repeats one image or phrase, and returns to it at the end, is easier to remember than one that lists facts.

### A president's speech

The book cites John F. Kennedy's May 1961 speech to Congress, in which he framed the space race as a contest America would lose if it did not make a new effort. About eight years later, on 20 July 1969, the United States became the first nation to put men on the moon. The book's point is that Kennedy created a sense of crisis, and a big, dramatic goal made people think in bigger ways.

## Storytelling techniques from the book

### 1. Pick a story that fits in a sentence

The red paperclip works because you can say it in one breath. Before you tell a longer story, try the short version. If you cannot say it in a sentence, you may not know yet what the story is.

### 2. Choose words that set the size of the problem

The book says our mindsets tend to be in proportion to the size of the problem. If it seems small, we think small. If it looks big, we think bigger. If it has crisis proportions, we apply all our creative energy. So the words you use to describe a problem change how people respond to it.

The chapter gives an example. It traces how the language for climate change moved over time between "global warming", "climate change" and "climate crisis", with different people recommending different words because each word makes the problem feel a different size. The chapter reports that some advisors argued the same word was right for opposite reasons. You do not have to take a side on that debate to see the point: the label is part of the story.

Ask of your own problem: what size does my wording make it feel? Would a different word make it feel more urgent, or less?

### 3. Dramatize it

The chapter says dramatization, used as a tool to solve problems, can force us to think a little bigger. It asks: how could you bring your business challenge to life, to inspire yourself and other stakeholders to crack it?

### 4. Exaggerate on purpose

The authors suggest exaggeration as a way to pull thinking away from the obvious solutions. In their traffic example, a mild exaggeration is that cars in Los Angeles are completely stationary: absolute gridlock, nobody can get to work. That extreme scenario shifts which solutions look sensible. Exaggeration is for finding ideas, not for reporting results. When you present facts to other people, state them plainly.

### 5. Give the idea a headline and a short news story

The Blast Off chapter asks you to give your idea an identity, including a punchy headline and a short news story saying what the idea is, what the point of it is and who it is aimed at. It is a good way to find out whether the story works. If you cannot write the headline, the idea is not ready to tell.

## How to tell your idea as a story

These steps are ours, built on the book's ideas.

1. **Say the idea in one sentence.** Aim for the red paperclip.
2. **Say whom it is for and what changes for them.** A story needs a person in it.
3. **Pick one vivid detail.** One specific image beats a list of features.
4. **Size the problem honestly.** Choose words that match how serious it really is.
5. **Write the headline.** If you cannot, go back to step 1.
6. **End with a next step.** Ask for something specific.

## Common mistakes

- **Letting the story outrun the facts.** The tagline is a joke. People who find out a story was inflated stop believing the next one.
- **Telling everything.** The red paperclip is one object and one idea.
- **Burying the point.** Say what the story is for in the first sentence.
- **Using big words for small problems.** If everything is a crisis, nothing is.
- **Forgetting the listener.** A story is for someone. Know who.

## Try it

1. Think of a business challenge you care about. Write it plainly in one sentence.
2. Rewrite it with two different words for the problem, one calmer and one more urgent, and notice how each changes what you would do.
3. Exaggerate the situation until it is absurd. Note what ideas show up.
4. Play the [Uses for a Paperclip game](/games/far-out/), the Far Out chapter's own exercise, to stretch your thinking before you write.
5. Write the headline and the three-sentence news story for your idea. The [Idea Launch Game](/games/blast-off/) walks you through it.

## Questions

**Is storytelling in business only for marketing?** No. The chapter's examples include a personal project, a government speech and a director charting a musical. The same skill helps when you pitch a colleague, a client or your boss.

**Is it all right to exaggerate?** In brainstorming, yes: exaggeration pulls your thinking away from obvious solutions. In what you tell other people, no. Keep the facts accurate.

**Do I need to be a good writer?** No. The red paperclip story is simple and works because the idea is clear.

**Where does this fit with a full storytelling course?** It does not replace one. This page covers what the book says. If you need structure for presentations or case studies, a dedicated course will go further.
