## What biomimicry is

Biomimicry means looking at how living things solve a problem and using what you learn to solve a human one. The word is built from Greek roots for life and imitation. A related word, biomimetics, is older, and people sometimes use the two interchangeably.

**It is not a method from the book.** Sources credit the writer Janine Benyus with bringing the word into wide use through her 1997 book *Biomimicry*, though who coined it is disputed, so treat that as the usual account. The book on this site never uses the word. What it does is closely related: it tells you to compare your problem to something else, and one of the places it points to is nature.

## What the book says

The Brain Flash chapter has a section on similes and metaphors. Its argument is that making comparisons helps highlight similarities and differences between things, and that this sparks ideas. It gives two comparisons from the natural world: a camera is like an eye, and an umbrella is like a canopy of leaves. Then it asks a question that you can use on any problem: what is your business challenge similar to, and does it remind you of something in nature or from another field?

Its longest example is about potato chips, and it is about leaves. As the book tells it, researchers at Procter and Gamble were stuck on how to pack chips so that they did not break. They thought about leaves. Dry leaves break apart easily, but leaves pressed together while they are moist and then left to dry have no gaps and do not break. That suggested packing the chips tightly in a tube. We have not checked the story independently. The point for this page is the move: they borrowed the way a natural thing behaves, not its look.

The Connected chapter gives the general reason this works. At bottom, it says, creativity is connecting two pieces that seem unconnected, and the resulting idea often looks obvious in hindsight.

## Three examples

These come from outside the book. We read several popular sources, and they agree on the outline and differ on the details, so we give the outline and leave out the numbers.

### Velcro

The usual account is that a Swiss engineer, George de Mestral, came back from a walk with his dog and found burrs stuck to his clothes and to the dog's fur. Looking at a burr, he saw that it was covered in tiny hooks. That gave him the idea for a fastener made of two strips, one with hooks and one with loops. Most accounts put the walk in 1941, though one gives a different year, and the patent dates in the 1950s vary between sources. The name is said to come from the French for velvet and hook.

What was borrowed: how a burr holds on, not what a burr looks like.

### A bullet train's nose

Some of Japan's early bullet trains made a loud boom when they came out of a tunnel at speed, which drew complaints from people living nearby. An engineer on the team for the 500 series, who was a bird-watcher, is credited with the idea of shaping the nose like a kingfisher's beak. By one account, the train went into service in 1997. Sources disagree on how much quieter and more efficient it was, so we give no figures.

What was borrowed: how a shape moves from one medium into another, not the look of a bird.

### Shark skin

Other sources cite shark skin, which is covered in tiny tooth-like scales, as the starting point for surface coatings and swimwear. We have not checked the details, and the claims about how well they work vary widely, so look at the source before you rely on it.

## How to borrow from nature

The book gives the habit. These steps are ours.

1. **Say what you need the thing to do.** Write the function in plain words: "stick two things together and let go cleanly", "enter a tunnel without a bang", "keep fragile things from breaking in a stack". Do not name the object.
2. **Ask who already does it.** What in nature has to do something like this? Think of animals, plants, seeds, shells and skin.
3. **Look at how, not what it looks like.** The chips were saved by how leaves behave when moist, not by a leaf shape.
4. **Write the principle in one plain sentence.** "A surface of tiny hooks will hold on to loops." Now it is no longer about a plant.
5. **Carry it back to your problem,** as the book does with its bakers and bread in the analogical thinking page.
6. **Check the comparison before you trust it.** See below.

### Questions to ask nature

| If you need to ... | Ask what in nature ... |
|---|---|
| Hold things together | clings, grips or sticks |
| Move through air or water quietly | moves through a fluid with little fuss |
| Protect something fragile | wraps, shells or cushions what it protects |
| Keep something clean | stays clean, or sheds what lands on it |
| Stay cool or warm | handles heat in a very hot or very cold place |

The table is ours and is only a set of prompts. The examples in the middle column are for you to find.

## Where it goes wrong

- **Copying the look.** A product that only looks like a leaf has borrowed nothing.
- **Assuming nature's answer fits your problem.** Nature solved its own problems, at its own scale and with its own materials.
- **Assuming nature's answer is better.** It is one source of ideas. It is not a guarantee.
- **Trusting a tidy story.** Origin stories about inventions are often simplified. The details of the examples above differ between sources.
- **Skipping the test.** An idea from nature still has to work in your setting.

## Questions

**What is biomimicry?** Using what you learn from how living things solve a problem to solve a human problem.

**What are some biomimicry examples?** Velcro, from burrs that stuck to a dog's fur, and a bullet train's nose shaped like a kingfisher's beak are the two most cited. The book's own nature comparison is the potato chip tube and the leaves.

**Is biomimicry in the book?** No. The book asks you to compare your problem to something else, including something in nature, and gives the chips and the umbrella as examples.

**Who coined the word?** Sources credit Janine Benyus with popularizing it in 1997, and they dispute who coined it. The older word biomimetics is also used.

**Is the Velcro story true?** The outline is consistent across sources: burrs, a dog and a walk. The year and the patent dates differ between sources, so we do not state them as fact.

**How do I try it?** Write what you need the thing to do, ask what in nature does something like it, and carry the principle back. Our estimate is about 15 minutes for the steps above.
