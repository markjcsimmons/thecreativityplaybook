## Get the problem right first

Most advice on solving problems goes straight to solutions. The Answer Is in the Question chapter does the opposite. Its idea is that if we get the problem right in the first place, we have a better chance of finding the best solution. The questions we ask shape the answers we get, so before spending your creative energy on a problem, check that it is the right problem.

The chapter gives four ways to do that. They work as a set, and you can use any one of them on its own.

## Technique 1: question your assumptions

Start by listing the assumptions you are making about the situation. Then ask whether each one holds up. The chapter's example is traffic congestion in Los Angeles, and the assumptions are things like "people need cars to get around" and "people need to travel around Los Angeles". The authors test each one. Do people need cars? They have legs, and there are buses and bicycles and the occasional train. Do they need to travel? Some journeys are essential, but many are for eating out, going to a movie or going to the beach.

One trick is to flip an assumption to its exact opposite and see whether it changes anything. If it makes a real difference, keep it in. If it makes none, you may be able to drop it.

## Technique 2: reframe the problem

How you frame a problem sends your creativity in a particular direction. In the traffic example, "reduce congestion" leads to roads and transit. "Reduce the need to travel" leads to working from home and local services. "Get people to use their cars less" leads to fees and incentives. The problem is the same and the solutions are different.

## Technique 3: split the problem into pieces

When a problem seems too big, break it up. The book says this has been used successfully in shipyards, car design and stage productions, and describes two methods.

**Splitting the cherry.** Write the problem in its simplest form, such as "traffic sucks". Split it into two pieces, such as the drivers and the roads. Split each of those into two more, such as "too many drivers" and "too few roads". Carry on until you cannot split any more, and you have a tree of small problems. From these you can choose which to focus on.

**The fishbone.** Kaoru Ishikawa developed this in the 1960s to manage work in the Kawasaki shipyards. You state the problem as simply as possible on the fish's head, write the key factors on the bones, then work through ways to address each one, one bone at a time.

## Technique 4: look through other eyes

The book's Different Strokes exercise looks at a problem as different people would see it. In the traffic example, a mayor, a police chief, an environmentalist and a commuter all agree that traffic is a problem, but for different reasons. The reason decides which solution is best. Buses might satisfy the environmentalist, for instance, but not the commuter, because buses make more stops and tend to be slower than cars.

The authors suggest assigning roles to a small team, giving them names and even props. It also works for getting buy-in: putting yourself in the shoes of the people who need to endorse your idea shows you their concerns before they raise them. The [Shoe Swapper game](/games/shoe-swapper/) plays this on screen.

## Creative problem solving examples

### Designing a sports car

The book tells how the designers of the Mazda MX-5 (also called the Miata) used the fishbone. They began with a design credo, a Japanese phrase that translates roughly as "rider and horse as one", about the driving experience of wind in your hair. They then broke the credo into five separate elements that together would deliver the car:

- compact and lightweight, but still safe,
- a cockpit big enough for two normal-sized adults,
- an engine placed so that weight is split 50:50 between the front and the rear,
- all four wheels used evenly to improve stability and performance, and
- a good connection between the engine and the rear differential, so the car responds to the throttle.

The book says the car stayed true to those five principles through three generations of design. A large goal (a feeling) became five things the designers could actually check.

### Charting a stage musical

In a sidebar, Dave Stewart describes a stage musical he worked on with the director Matthew Warchus. A musical is a massive undertaking, and when one thing changes it affects everything else. Warchus broke the whole show into a block diagram, with each block a scene. Blocks with the same colored border were connected by a musical thread. Looking at the whole show from a distance helped solve many issues, and it brought up Big Questions, such as what the musical was really about.

Warchus explained to the authors why a chart helps:

> The emotional effect of the story is achieved by patterns, and so only by creating a chart can you start to look at what the patterns are in the story, or what patterns you want to emphasise, or what patterns you want to add to the story.

*Matthew Warchus, director, in an interview with the authors. From Chapter 4 of the book.*

### A hotel's slow lifts

Not every example is from this chapter. In the Idea Spaghetti chapter, hotel guests complain about waiting for the lifts. A manager who thinks convergently hires an engineer, and a manager who thinks divergently looks for something else to do about the wait, such as giving guests magazines and mirrors. The [divergent vs convergent thinking](/techniques/divergent-vs-convergent-thinking/) page tells it in full.

## A six-step routine

The book gives the techniques, not a fixed process. This routine is ours, for combining them. Change it as you like.

1. **Write the problem simply.** One line, as bluntly as you like.
2. **List and test the assumptions.** Flip each to its opposite.
3. **Reframe it three ways.** Write each as "How can we ...?"
4. **Split it or see it through other eyes.** Use the cherry if it feels too big. Use Different Strokes if other people are involved.
5. **Make a pile of ideas.** Aim for quantity. The [Idea Spaghetti game](/games/idea-spaghetti/) is built for this.
6. **Choose and test.** Pick the best three and decide how to try the first one. The [Kill the Idea game](/games/kill-the-idea/) helps you cut a long list.

## Common mistakes

- **Solving the first version of the problem.** The book's whole chapter is a warning against this.
- **Skipping the assumptions.** Assumptions that go unchecked feel like facts.
- **Splitting forever.** Stop when you have pieces you can act on.
- **Using only your own viewpoint.** Others see problems you do not.
- **Going straight to the solution you like.** If you already love an answer, check whether you have asked the right question.

## Questions

**Is creative problem solving the same as brainstorming?** No. Brainstorming makes ideas. The techniques here work on the problem, so that the ideas you make are aimed at the right thing.

**Do these techniques work alone?** Yes. Question your assumptions and split the problem on paper. Different Strokes works best with a few people, though you can play the roles yourself.

**How long does it take?** The book does not say. A quick pass can take a few minutes, and a big problem can take a series of sessions.

**Is there a method with fixed steps?** The book's chapter describes techniques, not a fixed process. The six steps above are our suggestion.
