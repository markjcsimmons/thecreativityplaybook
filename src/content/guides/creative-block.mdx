## Being stuck is part of the process

Most advice on creative block treats it as a problem to fix as fast as possible. The book takes a different view. In the Left Brain, Meet Mr Right chapter, the director Matthew Warchus describes how he works with an assistant. He tells them to chip in when things are going well, and to say nothing when he is stuck:

> All I ask is that if I'm stuck don't help me. If things are going along really well then chip in, say what you want, but if I'm stuck don't say anything, because being stuck comes just before having a really brilliant idea.

He goes on:

> I think it's the magic moment creatively when you get a room full of people who are all stuck. I like that, it's a good sign.

*Matthew Warchus, director, in an interview with the authors. From Chapter 5 of the book.*

That does not make a block comfortable. But it changes what you do next. If being stuck is the stage before an idea, then the aim is not to force an answer. It is to give your mind something else to work with. The six ideas below, all from the book, do that.

## 1. Walk away on purpose

The same chapter describes a study. Students were given a creative task, such as coming up with uses for a brick. Some answered at once. Others spent a few minutes on a distracting task first. The distracted students came up with less obvious answers. The book's reading is that a break lets the mind wander and make connections that concentrated effort does not.

The book also says that music, video games and aerobic exercise change our mood, and mood changes how creatively we perform. So a break does not have to be idle. It can be a game, a song or a run.

The [Wheel of Distraction](/games/wheel-of-distraction/) is the book's board game for this. It picks a distraction at random. You do it properly, leave the problem alone, and then write down what pops into your head, however small or silly.

## 2. Throw down a banana skin

In the Connected chapter, the authors say that watching someone slip on a banana skin is funny because it makes a sudden connection between two unrelated things. They say the same trick can help when ideas are not flowing. Throw in a joke or a provocative image, and see how that disrupts thinking that is going stale or getting stuck in a rut.

It does not matter what it is, as long as it changes what is happening in a dramatic and sudden way. It brings in something bizarre, outrageous or irreverent, and it makes an odd connection between two ideas as a way to tell your brain to do the same with the problem. Try drawing a ridiculous version of your problem, or ask what the silliest possible answer would be.

## 3. Change the scene

The Connected chapter also has a sidebar by Dave Stewart about writers who get stuck. He describes writer's block as traumatizing, and as a vortex, because the more people worry about it the worse it gets. He says he has often helped people snap out of it by being humorous or crazy, or by taking them on an adventure. One of his favorites is a picnic in the countryside. He gets the writer involved in the shopping for the food, which takes their mind off the problem with the lyrics.

You do not have to be a songwriter to use this. Change the place, change the activity, and give the part of you that is worrying something else to do.

## 4. Force a random link

If you cannot find a way into your problem, let chance pick one. The Connected chapter suggests opening a dictionary at random and forcing a connection between the word you land on and your problem. The authors tried it on traffic congestion in Los Angeles and landed on "donation". They had ideas within a couple of minutes, including companies sponsoring free bus passes for commuters. They admit none of the ideas is amazing, but they point out that it got them past the initial barrier of having no obvious connection.

The [Random Idea Generator](/tools/random-idea-generator/) does the same job with a seeded word list. The book also mentions Oblique Strategies, a deck of playing cards by Brian Eno and Peter Schmidt that carry cryptic remarks and can help break a deadlock or solve a dilemma.

## 5. Lower the bar and make a ton of ideas

A block is often a quality problem in disguise: you are rejecting everything before you write it down. The Idea Spaghetti chapter says the more ideas we come up with, the more likely it is that one or two will be very good, and that the way to get many is to think divergently rather than converge on one answer. Its rule for brainstorms is that no idea is a bad idea.

So stop trying to write the good one. Write 20 bad ones. The [Idea Spaghetti game](/games/idea-spaghetti/) gives you a topic, a target and five minutes, which makes it hard to stop and judge.

## 6. Get unstuck together, but let people think alone first

Warchus says a room of stuck people is a good sign. The Idea Spaghetti chapter adds a warning: groups tend to produce more clichéd ideas than individuals do when many ideas are needed. So if a team is stuck, do not start by talking it through. Give everyone a few minutes alone, then compare lists.

## How to get out of a creative rut: a 15-minute routine

The book gives the techniques. This routine is our way of combining them, so change it freely.

1. **Name what you are stuck on (2 minutes).** Write it as one question.
2. **Walk away (5 minutes).** Do something absorbing and unrelated to the problem. Do not think about it.
3. **Throw in a banana skin (3 minutes).** Write the silliest possible answer. Draw it if you can.
4. **Make a quick pile (5 minutes).** Write as many ideas as you can, good or bad. Do not cross anything out.

Then leave the page. If nothing arrives, the book says the idea may turn up later. Keep the question somewhere you will see it again.

## Overcoming creative block: common mistakes

- **Staring harder.** The distraction study suggests the opposite works better.
- **Judging while you write.** The ideas that come out first are rarely the best, but they clear the way for the ones that are.
- **Treating a block as proof you are not creative.** The book treats being stuck as a normal stage, and in one director's case as a good sign.
- **Waiting for inspiration.** The six techniques above are things you do, not things you wait for.
- **Doing it alone when you need people, or with people when you need quiet.** Match the setting to the job.

## Questions

**Is creative block a real condition?** The book does not treat it as one, and neither do we. It describes being stuck as a normal part of making things.

**How long does a block last?** The book does not say. A short break may be enough, and some problems take longer. The advice is to keep the question in view and keep doing other things.

**What if nothing works?** Try a different technique, or a smaller version of the problem. If a block lasts a long time or is affecting your health, talk to someone you trust.

**Do I have to be a writer or a musician?** No. The book's examples come from songwriting, the theater and business. The techniques are the same for any problem.
