## How to use these exercises

Each exercise below is one of the board games from the book, written up so you can run it with a pen and paper. Each has a playable version on this site, linked at the end of its steps, if you would rather do it on screen.

The exercises fall into five jobs. Pick the one that matches what you need right now.

- **Make a lot of ideas.** Idea Spaghetti, the paperclip game and Inspiral.
- **Look at a problem differently.** What's Your Problem?, Shoe Swapper and Brain Flash.
- **Notice and recombine.** Attribute listing.
- **Get unstuck.** The Wheel of Distraction.
- **Choose and launch.** Kill the Idea and Blast Off.

The book keeps making ideas and judging them apart, and so do we. Do an exercise from the first four jobs before you reach for the last.

## Exercises for making a lot of ideas

### 1. Idea Spaghetti

**Chapter 2.** The book's argument is simple: the more ideas you come up with, the more likely it is that one of them is good. This exercise trains quantity, not judgment.

1. Pick a topic. The book's examples include "uses for a brick" and "new names for pasta". Or use a problem of your own.
2. Set a target for how many ideas you need in five minutes.
3. Write every idea you can think of, one per line. Do not stop to decide whether an idea is any good.
4. Score the round: a point for each idea over the target, a point lost for each one under it. Beat 12 with 15 ideas and you score 3.
5. Play four rounds, with a different topic and target each time.

The book suggests starting with its topics as a warm-up, then moving to real problems once you have the hang of making ideas fast. [Play Idea Spaghetti](/games/idea-spaghetti/).

### 2. The paperclip game

**Chapter 8.** This one is for stretching how far your ideas go. The chapter opens with a man who traded a single red paperclip, in a chain of swaps, for a house.

1. List as many uses for a paperclip as you can, one per line. There is no timer, and a longer list is better.
2. Check your list against the book's 25 common ideas, which run from a key-ring to an emergency wedding ring.
3. Score yourself: an idea on the list moves you one space and an idea not on the list moves you two. The book's rule is that you play against yourself, so everyone wins.

Be honest about what counts as on the list. If most people would have thought of it, it is on the list. [Play the paperclip game](/games/far-out/).

### 3. Inspiral

**Chapter 10.** The Connected chapter says creativity is about making connections between two things that seem unrelated. Inspiral forces that.

1. Roll the dice for two themes from the spiral, such as "health" and "space".
2. Set three minutes. Write as many ideas as you can that link the two themes. Sensible or crazy, it does not matter.
3. Count the unique ideas (exact repeats do not count) and move that many spaces toward the centre of the 36-space spiral.
4. Roll again. In the book you race other players. Alone, your score is how many turns it takes you to reach the centre.

To play with others, give everyone the same two themes, set three minutes and compare counts. [Play Inspiral](/games/inspiral/).

## Exercises for looking at a problem differently

### 4. What's Your Problem?

**Chapter 4.** The chapter's point is that the questions we ask shape the answers we get, so it is worth checking you are working on the right problem.

1. Write the problem as "How can we ...?" The book's example is "How can we sell more music?"
2. Roll a die for the kind of question: 1 is how, 2 is why, 3 is which, 4 is when, 5 is who and 6 is what.
3. Ask that kind of question about your problem and write your answer. The game has eight steps, so you do this several times.
4. At the end, rewrite the problem using what you found. Keep it simple and clear.

The rewrite is the result. It is often more specific than where you started. [Play What's Your Problem?](/games/whats-your-problem/).

### 5. Shoe Swapper

**Chapter 9.** To get your thinking moving in new directions, the chapter says, you need to leave what is familiar. This exercise does it by putting you in someone else's shoes.

1. Pick a problem you want ideas for.
2. Throw the dice to find whose shoes you are wearing, such as a young child, a fitness fanatic or a mad scientist.
3. Spend five minutes thinking as that person and write every idea they would have for your problem.
4. Throw again for new shoes and repeat. Play three rounds.
5. Choose the best idea, whichever pair of shoes it came from.

In the book's example, thinking as a celebrity led to celebrity gift bags for restaurant customers, and that led to a second idea of making every customer feel like a celebrity. [Play Shoe Swapper](/games/shoe-swapper/).

### 6. Brain Flash

**Chapter 6.** The chapter says visual stimuli help us make new ideas. The book's explanation is that pictures tap the creative side of the brain, which we treat as a way of talking, not as anatomy. The practical point stands: a forced comparison between a problem and a picture can show you something you would not see by thinking about the problem directly.

1. Decide on a problem, such as "How do we improve customer service?"
2. Throw two dice. The first counts along the bottom of the board and the second up the side, and that square holds a picture.
3. Complete the sentence: "It is like ... because ...". Then write the ideas the comparison sparks.
4. Play five rounds and keep your best idea from each.

The picture does not need to relate to the problem. That is the point. [Play Brain Flash](/games/brain-flash/).

## An exercise for noticing and recombining

### 7. Attribute listing

**Chapter 3.** The Scavengers chapter says there is a treasure trove of ideas lying around, and that this technique helps you find it. Break a product or service into its parts, then build it up again.

1. Name the thing: a pen, a restaurant, your own newsletter.
2. Pick three to five attributes that vary independently. For a restaurant, the book's chart uses the type of food, the price, the style and the way the food is delivered.
3. List the ordinary options and the odd ones under each heading.
4. Change one attribute at a time and ask whether the result is worth trying or a recipe for disaster. The chapter's own picks are expensive Italian food delivered to businesses, and mid-priced French food in a leisurely, family-friendly restaurant.

Change one thing at a time, so you can tell what the change did. [Try the Attribute Listing Tool](/tools/attribute-listing/).

## An exercise for getting unstuck

### 8. The Wheel of Distraction

**Chapter 5.** After you have worked on a problem for a while, the chapter says, your brain needs a rest from concentrated thought so it can wander and make less obvious connections. The book cites a study in which people who were distracted for a short time gave more original answers than people who kept thinking hard.

1. Spend a few minutes on a creative problem you are stuck on, then write it down.
2. Choose something absorbing and unrelated, such as a crossword, a bus ride or cooking. The game's wheel picks one at random.
3. Do it properly, and leave the problem alone.
4. Come back and write down whatever popped into your head, however small or silly.

If nothing comes, the book says the idea will turn up later. [Play the Wheel of Distraction](/games/wheel-of-distraction/).

## Exercises for choosing and launching

### 9. Kill the Idea

**Chapter 11.** Once you have a pile of ideas, half the job is choosing which ones deserve your time, money and energy. This exercise makes you choose.

1. Write down your strongest ideas, one per line, from three to nineteen. The book plays with 19.
2. Pick one at random. That idea is about to be killed.
3. Kill it or save it. You have three saves in total, and once two ideas are saved, the third save waits for the last round.
4. In sudden death, a coin toss picks which of your two saved ideas dies. Your gut tells you whether to spend the last save.

It does not rank your ideas. It shows you which one you would fight for. [Play Kill the Idea](/games/kill-the-idea/).

### 10. Blast Off

**Chapter 12.** The chapter says launching an idea matters as much as coming up with it. An idea on a scrap of paper is easy to leave there.

1. Choose one idea quickly, on gut instinct or at random. The clock starts now.
2. Give it a name and a look. Sketch it, however scrappy.
3. Write it up as a short news story with a punchy headline: what it is, what the point is and who it is for.
4. List the people, inside and outside your organization, who could help you launch it.
5. You have 30 minutes. At the end, you have a launch plan.

[Play Blast Off](/games/blast-off/).

## A 20-minute practice, built from these exercises

The book gives the exercises, not a practice schedule. This routine is ours, a suggestion for a short session you can repeat. Change the times to suit you.

1. **Warm up (5 minutes).** One round of Idea Spaghetti on a topic that has nothing to do with work.
2. **See it differently (10 minutes).** Take a real problem into What's Your Problem? or two rounds of Shoe Swapper.
3. **Choose (5 minutes).** Pick the best three ideas and write the next step for the first.

The habit all of these build is making many options before choosing one, which the [divergent vs convergent thinking](/techniques/divergent-vs-convergent-thinking/) page explains.

## Common mistakes

- **Judging while you make ideas.** The exercises that make ideas only work if you hold back criticism until the end.
- **Skipping the unpromising ones.** The book's Brain Flash and Inspiral prompts are meant to feel unrelated at first.
- **Stopping at the first idea.** Idea Spaghetti's targets are there to push you past the obvious ones.
- **Using only one kind of exercise.** Making, reframing and choosing use different muscles.
- **Never choosing.** A pile of ideas with no next step is a list. Finish with Kill the Idea or Blast Off.

## Questions

**What is the best creative thinking exercise for beginners?** Start with Idea Spaghetti. It has one rule, a clock and a score, and it gets you used to writing ideas without judging them.

**Can I do these alone?** Yes. The table shows how many people each one needs. Inspiral is a race against other players in the book, and several of the others work well with company.

**How long should I spend on them?** The book sets times for only some of them, and the table shows which. The timed ones run from three minutes a turn to 30 minutes for Blast Off.

**Do I need the book?** No. Each exercise here has the steps you need. The book gives the stories behind them.

**Are these exercises the same as lateral thinking?** They overlap. The book does not use the phrase, and our [lateral thinking](/lateral-thinking/) hub explains which chapters cover the same habits.
