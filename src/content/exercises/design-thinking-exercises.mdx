## What design thinking is, and what it is not here

Design thinking is a process, most often drawn as five stages: **empathize** with the people you are making something for, **define** the problem, **ideate** (make lots of ideas), **prototype** a rough version and **test** it. Other versions have three, four or six stages, and sources do not agree on the names.

**It is not a method from the book.** The book never uses the phrase. But Tim Brown, then head of the design firm IDEO, told the authors that once you reach a certain level of quality and efficiency there is little advantage in doing more of what you do, and that the alternative is new choices that did not exist before. That is what the stages are for, and the book's games cover most of them.

Where the book does not cover a stage, this page says so. It has no method for testing an idea with real users, for example, and nothing here pretends otherwise.

## Stage 1: Empathize

This stage is about understanding the people you are making something for before you start. The book's Shoe Swapper chapter says that the more we know about a subject, the more it can hold us back, and that looking through someone else's eyes gives a fresh view.

### Shoe Swapper

**Chapter 9.**

1. Pick the problem, and the person you are making it for.
2. Throw the dice to find whose shoes you are in: a young child, a fitness fanatic, a mad scientist.
3. Spend five minutes thinking as that person, and write what they would want, fear and do about your problem.
4. Throw again for new shoes. Play three rounds.
5. Look for what was the same in every pair of shoes. That is probably real.

The book's game uses roles and archetypes, not named people, and so should you. [Play Shoe Swapper](/games/shoe-swapper/).

### Look and listen

**Chapter 3, and ours.** The Scavengers chapter says ideas are lying around, and you find them by keeping your eyes open. Design thinking asks you to do that with the people you are designing for.

1. Choose one person, or one kind of person, who uses the thing you are making.
2. Watch them use it, or something like it, for ten minutes. Do not help.
3. Write down what they did, what they said and what surprised you.
4. Write one question you would like to ask them.

What people do and what people say they do are often different, so write both.

## Stage 2: Define

This stage is about getting the problem right. The book's Chapter 4 says get the problem right and you have a better chance of getting the answer right.

### What's Your Problem?

**Chapter 4.**

1. Write the problem as "How can we ...?"
2. Roll a die for the kind of question: 1 is how, 2 is why, 3 is which, 4 is when, 5 is who and 6 is what.
3. Ask that question about the problem and write your answer. Repeat, eight times in the book's game.
4. Rewrite the problem using what you found.

The rewrite is the result. [Play What's Your Problem?](/games/whats-your-problem/).

### Different Strokes

**Chapter 4.** The same problem looks different to different people with a stake in it. In the book's traffic example, a mayor, a police chief, an environmentalist and a commuter all agree it is a bad problem, but they care for different reasons, and the reason decides what a good solution needs.

1. Write the problem.
2. List four people with a stake in it, such as a customer, a supplier, someone who pays and someone who has to maintain it.
3. For each, write in one line what a good solution would need to do for them.
4. Rewrite the problem so that it includes the needs that are shared.

## Stage 3: Ideate

This is the stage the book is best at. It asks for a lot of ideas, with judgement held back until later.

### Idea Spaghetti

**Chapter 2.** The more ideas you make, the more likely one is good.

1. Take your rewritten problem from the last stage.
2. Set a target for how many ideas you want in five minutes.
3. Write every idea, one per line. Do not stop to judge.
4. Count them against the target, then play again with a higher one. [Play Idea Spaghetti](/games/idea-spaghetti/).

### Inspiral

**Chapter 10.** The Connected chapter says creativity comes from linking two things that seem unrelated.

1. Roll the dice for two themes from the spiral.
2. Spend three minutes writing ideas that connect them to your problem.
3. Count the unique ideas, and roll again. [Play Inspiral](/games/inspiral/).

### All Together Now

**Chapter 7.** For a group of four or more. Everyone writes ideas on a sheet for two minutes, passes it left, then builds on the sheet they receive. At the end each person gives 3, 2 and 1 points to their favorites. [Play All Together Now](/games/all-together-now/).

## Stage 4: Prototype

A prototype is a rough, cheap version you can show to someone. Evan Williams, a co-founder of Twitter, told the authors that he did not try to convince investors of the value of Twitter before it was a real product. His team built a prototype and used it themselves, and once they were using it, it was clear that it was interesting. The book's account is that the thing itself made the argument.

### Name it and sketch it

**Chapter 12, shortened.** The Blast Off! game starts by giving the idea a name and a look. That is a prototype of a kind.

1. Choose one idea. Do not spend long.
2. Give it a name.
3. Sketch it, however scrappy. Draw the thing, or the screen, or the room.
4. Write a one-line headline for it, as if it were in the news.
5. Show it to one person, without explaining it, and watch what they do.

[Play Blast Off!](/games/blast-off/).

## Stage 5: Test

The book covers the testing of an idea in two ways, and neither is the same as testing with real users. That needs a method from outside the book.

### Role-play the stakeholders

**Chapter 12.** Before you launch, the book asks you to put yourself in the shoes of everyone who could make or break the idea, and to ask of each of them:

1. What is in it for them?
2. Why would they help, or at least not stand in the way?
3. What would they see as a negative?
4. Does it conflict with something else they are trying to do?
5. Does it make their life harder?

The book's point is that empathizing with the key stakeholders lets you find ways to remove problems before they ever see them.

### Kill the Idea

**Chapter 11.** Write down your strongest ideas, pick one at random, and decide whether to kill it or spend one of your three saves on it. The choice shows you which idea you would fight for. It is a test of your own conviction, not of whether the idea will work for anyone else. [Play Kill the Idea](/games/kill-the-idea/).

## A problem followed through all five stages

The book's running problem is traffic in Los Angeles. This walk-through is ours and uses ideas the book itself reaches for the problem.

- **Empathize.** Think as a commuter, a bus driver and someone who works from home. The commuter wants a journey that is shorter and less stressful. The person who works from home wants no journey at all.
- **Define.** "How can people in Los Angeles spend less time stuck in traffic?" The commuter's view shifts the question to "How can people spend less time traveling?", which includes not traveling.
- **Ideate.** Five minutes of Idea Spaghetti gives car pooling, car pool lanes, shared minibuses, staggered start times and a day a week without traveling.
- **Prototype.** Sketch a car pool lane notice, or write the one-page offer to staff for a day a week without traveling.
- **Test.** Role-play the employer: what is in it for them, what would they see as negative? Then try the offer with one team.

## Common mistakes

- **Skipping the first stage.** People often go straight to ideas, because ideas are what the book teaches. The ideas are better if you have looked at the problem through other eyes first.
- **Treating the stages as a straight line.** Most versions loop. Testing often sends you back to the problem.
- **Prototyping too late, or too well.** A rough sketch that you can show today is worth more than a polished plan next month.
- **Mistaking the games for testing.** Role-playing a stakeholder and choosing between ideas are useful, but they are not the same as watching someone use the thing.
- **Naming real people.** Use roles and types, as the book's games do, not people you know.

## Questions

**What are design thinking exercises?** Activities that help a team move through the stages of design thinking: understand people, define the problem, make ideas, build a rough version and test it.

**Is design thinking in the book?** No. The book never uses the phrase. Its games fit the stages, and the page says which game goes where.

**How many stages are there?** One common version has five: empathize, define, ideate, prototype and test. Other versions have three, four or six, and the names differ.

**What is an example of a design thinking exercise?** Role-playing someone else's shoes to understand a problem is one. The Shoe Swapper game and the stakeholder role-play from the Blast Off! chapter both do it.

**Does the book cover testing?** Not testing with real users. It covers role-playing the people who could make or break an idea, and choosing between ideas with Kill the Idea.

**Can I do these alone?** Most, yes. All Together Now needs four or more people, and Different Strokes works better with two or more.
