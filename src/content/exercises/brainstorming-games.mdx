## Write alone first, then share

Most group brainstorms start with everyone talking. The book suggests a better order. In the Idea Spaghetti chapter it cites a study, by Leigh Thompson, that found groups are better at convergent thinking (narrowing down) and individuals are better at divergent thinking (making many ideas). The creativity quiz at the end of the Blast Off chapter goes further: group brainstorms are sometimes counterproductive, with fewer and worse ideas than the same people would make alone.

The book also says what helps: setting the goals very high, and making the mix of people less alike. The games below follow from that. Most of them have a stage where people write on their own, and then a stage where the group shares, builds or chooses. Putting the solo stage first is our reading of the evidence, not a rule in the book.

The [divergent vs convergent thinking](/techniques/divergent-vs-convergent-thinking/) page explains the difference.

## Names for these games

People search for these under several names: brainstorming exercises, creative thinking games, "gamestorming" and "innovation games". They all mean a group exercise with a structure that helps people make ideas. The book uses none of those words. It calls its own exercises board games, because each one began as a game on a board.

## Ground rules for any group game

These come from the book's rules for a perfect brainstorm in the Idea Spaghetti chapter. Say them out loud before you start.

- **No idea is a bad idea.** The point is to let every idea out and give it a chance to breathe.
- **No heckling.** Judging comes later, in a separate step.
- **Build on each other's ideas.** The book says to reward the group as a whole, so people add to each other's ideas instead of trying to make sure theirs wins.
- **Set clear parameters.** Agree the question, the time limit and the target before you start.
- **Mix the people.** The book asks for people from different backgrounds, because people with similar ideas and beliefs tend to produce similar ideas.
- **Add a little competition.** A high benchmark, such as the number of ideas another group made, brings out the competitive spirit.
- **Pick an informal setting.** A space without too much formality or rigidity helps.

The [brainstorming template](/templates/brainstorming-template/) puts these on one printable page.

## The games

### 1. All Together Now (brainwriting)

**Chapter 7.** This is the book's group game, and it takes the solo-then-share order to its limit. Brainwriting means brainstorming on paper: everyone writes, and the sheets move round the group. The book does not use that word. It compares the game to Chinese Whispers (Telephone, in the US), where a phrase changes as it passes from person to person. Here the change is the point.

1. Four or more of you agree a creative challenge.
2. Everyone writes ideas on their own sheet for two minutes, leaving room underneath.
3. Pass your sheet to the person on your left. On the sheet you receive, write new ideas triggered by the ones already there.
4. Keep passing every two minutes, until each sheet is back with the person who started it.
5. Take turns reading out your sheet. Each player has 6 points to give: 3 for a favorite idea, 2 for a second and 1 for a third.
6. The idea with the most points wins.

The book ends on the line that love is more important than winning. The vote is for finding the best idea, not for producing a loser. [Run the timer and the vote with the Brainwriting game](/games/all-together-now/).

### 2. Idea Spaghetti race

**Chapter 2.** The book's board game is for one to four players, each with their own fork. It is a quantity game, with a target and five minutes a round.

1. Everyone gets the same topic and the same target for how many ideas they need in five minutes.
2. Everyone writes alone, one idea per line, with no talking.
3. Score the round: a point for each idea over the target, a point lost for each under it.
4. Compare sheets. Read out the best ideas, and let others add to them.
5. Play four rounds with a new topic each time.

The book's rules are the topic, the target, the five minutes and the scoring. The steps for comparing sheets afterward are ours. To play with others on screen, deal yourselves the same topic and compare scores. [Play Idea Spaghetti](/games/idea-spaghetti/).

### 3. Inspiral race

**Chapter 10.** In the book, Inspiral is a race: players take turns, and the first to the centre of the spiral wins. It works as a group warm-up because everyone is writing at once.

1. Roll the dice for two themes from the spiral.
2. Everyone has three minutes to write ideas that link the two themes, however sensible or crazy.
3. Count your unique ideas. Exact repeats do not count.
4. The most unique ideas moves furthest toward the centre.

Roll again for new themes. [Play Inspiral](/games/inspiral/).

### 4. Brain Flash collage

**Chapter 6.** When the problem is big, split it into pieces, one for each person. The book says somewhere between four and eight people is usually a good number, and adds that it depends on how many comfortable chairs you have.

1. Divide the problem into as many parts as there are people.
2. Each person sketches a solution to their part.
3. Everyone shows their sketch. The others add ideas to improve it, with no heckling.
4. Put the sketches together as a collage on a wall or table. Look for new ideas in how the pieces fit.

The book's reason for sketching is that pictures can bring ideas that words do not. We treat its talk of left and right brains as a figure of speech. [Play Brain Flash on screen](/games/brain-flash/) for the picture-comparison version.

### 5. Building with blocks

**Chapter 7.** The book calls this building with blocks, and says the technique is known as successive element integration. It turns a pile of small ideas into one bigger, developed idea that everyone has a stake in.

1. Collect a bunch of small ideas, one per card or sticky note.
2. Take one idea and add it to a second to form a third.
3. Add another idea to the third to form a fourth. Keep going until the stack is about to topple.

The book's example is Los Angeles traffic. "Make all lanes car pool lanes" plus "build more roads" gives "build whole roads, not just lanes, for people who car pool". Add "improve public transport" and you get "charge people who don't car pool and use the money to fund better public transport". Add "ask businesses to incentivise employees to car pool" and the stack ends at luxury minibuses with wi-fi and coffee that run in the car pool lane, so staff start work before they reach the office.

The book's point is that the group might have reached a similar answer another way, but accepting that each idea plays some part makes it a process all participants feel ownership of.

While you build, borrow the Chapter 7 habit: when you hear an idea, start by saying "What I like about this idea is ..." and name at least one good thing about it. The book says the analytical side of the brain is often too keen to start editing before the creative side has had a chance, and that finding positives first makes the person feel good about sharing next time.

### 6. Different Strokes

**Chapter 4.** This is close to Shoe Swapper, with set roles for a team, and it comes from the problem-solving chapter. In the book, a mayor, a police chief, an environmentalist and a commuter all agree that traffic is a problem. They disagree on why, and the reason decides which solution is best.

The steps are ours, built on the book's description.

1. State the problem in one sentence.
2. Give each person a role, a name and, if you like, a prop. The authors suggest this for a small team.
3. Each person writes their version of the problem from their role's point of view, then two or three ideas for it.
4. Read them out and compare. Which ideas suit which reason? Which ones would a different role reject?

It also works for buy-in. Putting yourself in the shoes of the people who must approve an idea shows you their concerns before they raise them. [Play Shoe Swapper](/games/shoe-swapper/) for the solo version.

### 7. Kill the Idea with a team

**Chapter 11.** The book's game is about your own ideas, and the book does not give a team version. This adaptation is ours.

1. Put the team's strongest ideas on a list, from three to nineteen.
2. Pick one at random. That idea is about to be killed.
3. The team may spend a save to rescue it. Agree beforehand who decides, or take turns.
4. Play to sudden death. The idea that ends with two saves is the one the team would fight for.

The game does not rank the ideas. It makes the team choose, and the choice shows what people care about. [Play Kill the Idea](/games/kill-the-idea/).

## Tips for running a group game

- **Watch your own behavior as the leader.** The Chapter 7 research the book cites found that a leader's negative behaviors had more effect than positive ones. The ones it names include giving out assignments without understanding people's capabilities, micromanaging and not dealing with problems. In a game, the same goes for sighing at an idea.
- **Know when not to ask for ideas.** The book says good leaders know when to look for new ideas and when not to. If the decision is made, say so and explain the vision instead.
- **Try it early.** The book's director, Matthew Warchus, gets people on their feet on the first day so that nobody fears the moment of starting. The lesson for business is not to wait too long before trying out an idea in some form.
- **Playing online.** This is our suggestion, not the book's: use a shared document or whiteboard with one page per person for the writing stages, and pass pages by reassigning them. The rules and the order matter more than the tool.

## Common mistakes

- **Starting with a group discussion.** The first idea said out loud tends to shape the rest.
- **Judging too early.** One sigh and people stop offering ideas.
- **Playing to win.** The book says love is more important than winning. The vote is for finding the best idea.
- **A team that is too alike.** The book says a mix of people helps, because like minds tend to make like ideas.
- **No next step.** End by picking three ideas and naming who does what next.

## Questions

**What is the best brainstorming game for a team?** For four or more people, All Together Now is the book's own group game. It has a clock, a rule and a vote, and everyone writes at once.

**What is brainwriting?** Brainstorming on paper. Instead of calling out ideas, everyone writes them down, and the sheets move round the group. The book calls the game All Together Now and does not use the word.

**How many people do I need?** The book's All Together Now needs four or more. Idea Spaghetti is for one to four players. The Brain Flash collage works with four to eight.

**Are these the same as lateral thinking games?** They overlap. The book does not use the phrase, and our [lateral thinking](/lateral-thinking/) hub explains which chapters cover the same habits.

**Can we play these online?** Yes. The [games](/games/) have on-screen versions, and the writing games work with a shared document.
