## What an ideation workshop is

An ideation workshop is a session with a clear problem, a mixed group and a plan for making ideas and picking some. The book does not use the phrase, and it has no workshop format of its own. What it does have is a set of exercises, each of which does one job: warming up, reframing a problem, making ideas, building on them, and choosing. This page strings them together.

**The agenda below and its times are ours.** The exercises, the rules and the evidence are the book's.

## Before the workshop

The book's rules for a perfect brainstorm, in the Idea Spaghetti chapter, double as a checklist for the planning.

1. **Write the problem as a question.** The Answer Is in the Question chapter says to start "How can we ...?" and to keep it simple and clear. Do it before the day. The workshop's first block will revisit it.
2. **Fix the parameters.** Decide the time limit, the target number of ideas and the question. The book asks for clear parameters.
3. **Mix the people.** The book asks for people from different backgrounds, because people with similar ideas and beliefs tend to produce similar ideas. In a sidebar, Dave Stewart makes the same point about meetings: include outsiders, and do not confine discussions to insiders who think in exactly the same way.
4. **Choose an informal room.** A space without too much formality or rigidity helps, the chapter says.
5. **Set a high bar.** The book suggests a high benchmark, such as the number of ideas another group made, to bring out healthy competition.
6. **Gather the materials.** Paper and pens for everyone, a timer, a wall or whiteboard, and sticky notes. The list is ours.

For a group of four to eight, the book's rules fit without change. All Together Now needs four or more, and the book's collage exercise suggests four to eight. For a bigger group, split into teams of that size. Splitting is our suggestion.

## The 90-minute agenda

### Block 1: Warm up (10 minutes)

Run one round of [Idea Spaghetti](/games/idea-spaghetti/) on a topic that has nothing to do with work, such as "uses for a brick". Everyone writes alone for five minutes against a target. The book says to start with its topics as a warm-up before moving to real problems. Use the other five minutes to read out the funniest ideas, which lowers the stakes for what comes next.

### Block 2: Reframe the problem (15 minutes)

Hand out the problem you wrote beforehand. In pairs, use the six question words from [What's Your Problem?](/games/whats-your-problem/): how, why, which, when, who and what. Ask each kind of question about the problem and write the answers. Each pair then rewrites the problem as "How can we ...?"

Put the rewrites on the wall and pick one for the group to work on. The book's point is that the questions we ask shape the answers we get, so the group should agree on the question before it makes any ideas.

### Block 3: Make ideas (20 minutes)

Run [All Together Now](/games/all-together-now/). Everyone writes alone for two minutes, passes the sheet left, adds ideas triggered by the sheet they receive, and keeps passing until each sheet returns to its owner. Then read out and vote, with 3, 2 and 1 points each.

This is the solo-then-share order the book's evidence supports: individuals do better at making ideas, and the passing adds the group's contribution. Each pass takes two minutes and there is one pass per person, so a group of six should expect roughly 12 minutes of writing before the read-out, which is why this block gets 20 minutes. For eight people, give it more time or split into two groups.

### Block 4: Break (10 minutes)

Stop. The Left Brain, Meet Mr Right chapter says that after you have worked on a problem for a while, your brain needs a rest from concentrated thought so that it can wander and make less obvious connections. Spin the [Wheel of Distraction](/games/wheel-of-distraction/), or pick something absorbing yourselves, and do it properly. A cup of coffee while you talk about the problem is not a break.

### Block 5: Build (15 minutes)

Take the best-voted ideas and use the Chapter 7 technique, building with blocks. Add one idea to a second to make a third, then add a fourth to the third, and so on. The book says the result is something every participant feels ownership of, because each idea has played some part.

While you build, use the book's rule: when you hear an idea, start by saying "What I like about this idea is ..." and name something good in it. It keeps editing from jumping in too soon.

### Block 6: Choose (15 minutes)

Run [Kill the Idea](/games/kill-the-idea/) on the stacked ideas, with the team deciding who spends the saves. Agree the rule for the saves beforehand. The book says that half the job, once you have a pile of ideas, is choosing which of them get your time, money and energy. The game does not rank the ideas. It shows which one people would fight for.

### Block 7: Next steps (5 minutes)

Name the idea. List who could help launch it, inside and outside the organization: the Blast Off chapter calls this the crew. The book says it is almost impossible to do it alone. Put a name and a date next to each action before anyone leaves the room.

For the follow-up, [Blast Off](/games/blast-off/) gives you 30 minutes to turn one idea into a launch plan: a name and a sketch, a short news story with a headline, and a crew.

## Icebreaker games for meetings

You do not need a full workshop to open a meeting with an exercise. These three games from the book run in about five minutes each, and none needs a facilitator.

- **Idea Spaghetti.** One round: a topic, a target and five minutes of writing alone. [Play it](/games/idea-spaghetti/).
- **Inspiral.** Two themes and three minutes to link them. Compare counts with the person next to you. [Play it](/games/inspiral/).
- **The paperclip game.** Uses for a paperclip, then compare against the book's list. [Play it](/games/far-out/).

The book does not call these icebreakers. We use them that way because each is quick, has a clear start and end, and gets people writing before they talk.

## A shorter and a longer version

**A 45-minute workshop.** Skip the warm-up and the break, and keep a shorter version of each block. Reframe (10 minutes), make ideas (15), choose (10), next steps (10).

**A half-day workshop.** Keep the 90-minute agenda. Add [Shoe Swapper](/games/shoe-swapper/) or the Different Strokes exercise after the first round of ideas, so people look at the problem from other roles. Add the Brain Flash collage for a big problem, split into as many parts as there are people. The [brainstorming games](/exercises/brainstorming-games/) page has both.

**Over weeks.** The Blast Off chapter describes a slower way: give the group a notebook and one problem, ask for one idea a day for four weeks, add new information every few days, and finish with each person's best idea and some suggestions. It also describes monthly idea jams, where people share ideas from their notebooks with no set problem.

## Leading the session

- **Watch your own behavior.** The research behind Chapter 7 found that a leader's negative behaviors had more effect than positive ones. The ones it names include giving out assignments without understanding people's capabilities and micromanaging. A raised eyebrow at an idea counts.
- **Champion ideas that are not yours.** The book says good leaders do not see themselves as the source of ideas that others carry out. It says to manage for creativity, not to try to manage it.
- **Know when not to ask.** Good leaders know when to look for new ideas and when not to. If the decision is made, say so.
- **Reduce the fear of failure.** The book's director, Matthew Warchus, gets people on their feet on the first day, because the longer you put it off, the more daunting the first try becomes. The lesson for business is to try an idea out, in some form, before the fear grows. His advice for a rehearsal room is a zigzag between thinking and play.
- **Invite different kinds of people.** The book tells of Nokia's Ideas Camp, which invited really different types of people together:

> Our Ideas Camp is a step in that direction. We invite really different types of people together with an agenda that gets formed on the spot.

*Tero Ojanperä, then an Executive Vice-President at Nokia, in an interview with the authors. From Chapter 12 of the book (2010 edition).*

## Remote and hybrid

The following is our suggestion, not the book's. Use a shared document with one page per person for the writing stages, and pass pages by reassigning them. Keep each writing round short, and have everyone read the same screen during the vote. The rules and the order matter more than the tool.

## Common mistakes

- **No problem statement.** A group handed a vague topic makes vague ideas. Write the question beforehand.
- **Everyone talks first.** The first idea said out loud tends to shape the rest. Write alone, then share.
- **Skipping the choosing.** Ideas with no choice at the end become a list. Pick three and name who does what.
- **Never stopping.** The break is part of the plan.
- **All the same kind of people.** The book's rule is a mix of people from different backgrounds.
- **A leader who judges.** One sigh is enough to stop the flow.

## Questions

**How long should an ideation workshop be?** The book does not say. The agenda above runs 90 minutes, which is our choice, and the shorter version runs 45.

**How many people do I need?** Four or more for All Together Now. The book's collage exercise suggests four to eight. For more, split into teams.

**Do I need a facilitator?** One person should hold the clock and the rules. Because a leader's behavior has so much effect (see the section above), our advice is to choose someone who will not judge ideas.

**What if people will not join in?** Start with the solo writing. The sheets hand ideas to the group without anyone having to present them, so nobody has to speak first.

**Is this the same as design thinking?** No. The book does not use the phrase, and the agenda is built from its own exercises.
