## What this page is for

Search for problem solving activities and most of what you find are team-building games: build a tower, escape a room, cross an imaginary river. They are good for a group that needs to get to know each other. This page is for a different job. You have a real problem, and you want to understand it before you start proposing fixes.

All of the activities below come from the book. The session plan that puts them together, and the times, are ours.

## The activities

| Activity | What it does | Chapter | Time | People |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Test your assumptions | Finds what you take for granted | 4 | 8 minutes | 1 or more |
| What's Your Problem? | Reframes the problem six ways | 4 | 10 minutes | 1 or more |
| Different Strokes | Sees the problem from other seats | 4 | 10 minutes | 2 to 4 |
| Split it up | Breaks a big problem into pieces | 4 | 8 minutes | 1 or more |
| Sketch it | Pictures now, vision and barriers | 6 | 15 minutes | 1 or more |

The times are ours, and the book sets none for these.

### 1. Test your assumptions

List the assumptions you are making about the problem, and then ask whether each one holds up. The book's example is Los Angeles traffic. People assume that they need cars to get around and that they need to travel at all. Both are tested: people have legs, buses and bicycles, and many journeys are for things like eating out or going to the beach. A trick from the book is to flip an assumption to its exact opposite and see whether it changes anything. If it does, it matters. If not, you may be able to drop it.

### 2. What's Your Problem?

Write the problem as "How can we ...?" Then ask it six kinds of question: how, why, which, when, who and what. The book plays this with a die. Each answer gives a new angle, and at the end you rewrite the problem. You can [play the game on screen](/games/whats-your-problem/) for a solo run.

### 3. Different Strokes

This is the book's exercise for seeing a problem as other people see it. A mayor, a police chief, an environmentalist and a commuter all agree that traffic is a problem, but for different reasons, and the reason decides which solution is best. Give each person in the group a role. The authors suggest names and even props. Each person writes the problem as their role sees it.

### 4. Split it up

When a problem seems too big, the book says to break it up. It describes two ways. In one, you split the problem in two, then split each half in two again, and carry on until you cannot split any more. In the other, a fishbone, you put the problem at the head of a fish, write the main factors on the bones and work through each one. The [creative problem solving](/guides/creative-problem-solving/) guide has both in full.

### 5. Sketch it

The Brain Flash chapter has a drawing method. Sketch how things are now on one side of a page and how you want them to be on the other. Then sketch the barriers in a second color, and ideas for breaking them down in a third. The steps are in the book's Brain Flash chapter.

## A 45-minute session

This plan is ours. It is for one problem, with a group of up to six or alone. Use a timer.

| Minutes | Step | Activity |
|---|---|---|
| 5 | Write the problem | One line, as bluntly as you like. Everyone writes it alone first. |
| 8 | Test the assumptions | Each person lists three assumptions. Flip each one to its opposite. |
| 10 | Reframe it | Ask the six questions and write the problem two new ways. |
| 10 | Change seats | Different Strokes: two or three roles, each writes the problem their way. |
| 7 | Split or sketch | Split the problem into pieces, or sketch it. Pick one. |
| 5 | Choose | Pick the version of the problem you will work on, and one next step. |

At the end you have a rewritten problem, a list of assumptions that did not hold, a few viewpoints and a next step. You do not have a solution, and that is deliberate. The book's argument is that getting the problem right gives you a better chance of getting the answer right. Solutions are the next session. See the [brainstorming template](/templates/brainstorming-template/) for it.

### Running it alone

Skip Different Strokes, or play both sides of a disagreement yourself. Swap it for the [Shoe Swapper](/games/shoe-swapper/) game, which deals the viewpoint.

### Running it with a group

Everyone writes alone before anyone speaks, and then you compare. The book says individuals are better at making a variety of ideas, and groups are better at choosing between them. See [team workshop activities](/exercises/team-workshop-activities/) for a longer agenda.

## Not the same as team-building

The popular lists mix two things. Team-building games build trust and communication, and the problem is made up. These activities work on a problem you really have. If you want puzzles for fun, the [lateral thinking puzzles](/lateral-thinking/puzzles/) are better for that, and they have answers.

## Common mistakes

- **Jumping to solutions.** The session ends before any are offered. That is the point.
- **Skipping the assumptions.** An assumption that goes unchecked feels like a fact.
- **Choosing roles that agree.** Pick viewpoints that disagree, such as the person who pays and the person who uses.
- **Using real people in roles.** Use job roles and types, as the book does.
- **No next step.** Write one before you leave.

## Questions

**What are problem solving activities for adults?** Exercises that help a person or a group understand and reframe a real problem. This page has five from the book and a 45-minute plan.

**Are these team-building games?** No. Team-building games practice teamwork on an invented task. These work on a problem you really have.

**Can I do them alone?** Yes. Most work for one person. Different Strokes works best with a few people, but you can play the roles yourself.

**How long do they take?** The five take 8 to 15 minutes each, by our estimate. The session plan runs 45 minutes.

**Do they solve the problem?** They get the problem right. The book's view is that this makes a good answer more likely, and making ideas is the next step.

**Where do the activities come from?** The book's Chapter 4 and Chapter 6. The session plan and the times are ours.
