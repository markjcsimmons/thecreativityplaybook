## The proposals in full

### New evidence, examples and techniques

**C1-01 (New evidence, Core)**  
*Where:* "What's the big idea?", after the pull quote "Creativity is more than the production of ideas..."

*Proposed:* Researchers have used the same two-part test since the 1950s: is it original, and does it work?

*Why:* Backs the book's own definition with the research standard.

*Evidence:* Runco & Jaeger (2012), "The Standard Definition of Creativity", Creativity Research Journal 24(1), 92-96. Traces the originality-plus-effectiveness definition to at least 1953. *Confidence: High.*

**C1-02 (New evidence, Optional)**  
*Where:* "What's the big idea?", after "...they got better and better at it until, at last, they were able to produce truly original songs..."

*Proposed:* Practice is putting it mildly. During one 1961 residency at Hamburg's Top Ten Club the band played 92 nights, from seven in the evening until two in the morning on weekdays and later at weekends: about 500 hours on stage.

*Why:* Gives the "practise being creative" point a number. Remove if you want to avoid a figure that rests on one secondary source.

*Evidence:* Beatles Bible (Top Ten Club, 1961: 92 nights, 503 hours on stage; 7pm-2am weekdays, 8pm-4am weekends). Secondary source; check against Mark Lewisohn, Tune In. *Confidence: Medium.*

**C1-03 (Replace study, Core)**  
*Where:* "Creativity is child's play", Robinson paragraph (the 98/10/2 percent figures and footnote 7)

*Current:* In fact, data shows how as children get older they lose their ability to think divergently (see Chapter 2), which is a key component of creativity: 98 percent of three- to five-year-olds tested showed they could think in divergent ways, but by the time they were 13 to 15 years old, only 10 percent could think in this way. And when the test was used with 25-year-olds, only 2 percent could think divergently.

*Proposed:* Coming up with lots of different answers to one question is called divergent thinking (see Chapter 2), and it is a key part of creativity. The data backs Robinson up, at least in part. Psychologist Kyung Hee Kim analysed nearly 300,000 scores on the Torrance Tests of Creative Thinking, collected between 1968 and 2008. Since 1990, while IQ scores rose, creative thinking scores fell, and the steepest drop was among the youngest children, from kindergarten to third grade. Kim is careful not to say what caused it.

*Why:* The 98/10/2 figures come from a 1960s study that, as far as we can tell, was never published in a peer-reviewed journal and is retold differently in different places (ages 3-5 or 4-5). Kim's analysis is published, much larger and more recent. New footnote 7 would cite Kim (2011).

*Evidence:* Kim (2011), analysis of about 300,000 Torrance Tests of Creative Thinking scores, 1968-2008 (William & Mary news release, wm.edu/news/stories/2011/smart-yes-creative-not-so-much.php). Journal details to verify. *Confidence: Medium (journal details to verify).*

**C1-04 (New evidence, Core)**  
*Where:* "Can creativity be rediscovered?", after the pull quote about late bloomers

*Proposed:* The business world agrees. A US Census Bureau study of more than 2.7 million company founders found that the founders behind the fastest-growing new firms, the top one in a thousand, were 45 on average.

*Why:* Replaces a 1991 reference with a modern, large-scale finding that supports the late-bloomer point.

*Evidence:* Azoulay, Jones, Kim & Miranda, "Age and High-Growth Entrepreneurship", American Economic Review: Insights 2020 (NBER w24489): mean age 45.0 for the top 1 in 1,000 fastest-growing new firms; 42 across all founders; 2.7 million founders, US Census data. *Confidence: High.*

**C1-05 (New evidence, Core)**  
*Where:* "The Pyramid of the Powerless", after "...so the idea dies before it ever gets to the decision-maker."

*Proposed:* Harvard professor Amy Edmondson has a name for what is missing: psychological safety, the belief that you will not be punished or humiliated for speaking up with an idea, a question or a mistake. Her 1999 study of workplace teams found it helped teams learn, and when Google went looking for what made its own teams successful, it reported that psychological safety mattered most.

*Why:* The chapter describes the problem (naysayers) but not the research on the fix.

*Evidence:* Edmondson (1999), Administrative Science Quarterly 44(2), 350-383. Google Project Aristotle coverage: Inc., World Economic Forum, NYT Magazine (Duhigg, Feb 2016, to verify). *Confidence: Medium (Google figures vary by source).*

**C1-07 (New example, Core)**  
*Where:* "What's the big idea?", after the Edison paragraph (novel and useful)

*Proposed:* Sometimes the useful part arrives disguised as a joke. In 2008 three broke founders were trying to keep an air-bed rental idea alive. During the US election they designed novelty cereal boxes, "Obama O's" and "Cap'n McCain's", and sold them for $40 a box. The cereal raised about $30,000, paid off their credit card debts and caught the eye of Y Combinator's Paul Graham. The company was Airbnb.

*Why:* A post-2010 example of a playful idea that turned out to be useful. Fits the voice of the book.

*Evidence:* CNBC 18 Apr 2023 and Fortune 19 Apr 2023 (Brian Chesky): election-themed cereal boxes at $40, about $30,000 raised, paid off debts, impressed Y Combinator's Paul Graham. Figures vary between sources. *Confidence: Medium (figures vary: check the number of boxes and exact use of funds).*

**C1-08 (New technique, Core)**  
*Where:* After "Saying creativity is child's play..." section, as a boxed exercise

*Proposed:* TRY IT: YES, AND. Improvisers have one golden rule: accept what your partner says, then add to it. In 2013 researchers at the University of Hertfordshire found that people who did a short improvisation task scored higher on a standard test of divergent thinking (fluency, originality and flexibility) than people who did a control task. It was a small, early study, so treat it as a promising nudge, not proof. Time: 5 minutes. Group: 2 or more. Materials: none. 1. Pair up and pick a silly starting idea, such as "Let's open a restaurant on the moon." 2. Take turns. Every sentence must start with "Yes, and..." and add something new. 3. No "but", no "no". Keep going for three minutes. 4. Stop and write down the five best things you came up with. Use it as a warm-up before any brainstorm.

*Why:* The chapter is about play but has no exercise to play with. This one needs no materials and has a research basis.

*Evidence:* Lewis & Lovatt (2013), Thinking Skills and Creativity 9, 46-58, University of Hertfordshire. Two experiments (verbal and music improvisation) raised fluency, originality and flexibility on the Alternative Uses Task versus a control task. Early, small-scale evidence; a 2018 review found little evidence for wider improv benefits. *Confidence: Medium.*

**C1-09 (New technique, Optional)**  
*Where:* After the Moon and Sixpence section or near the end of "Can creativity be rediscovered?"

*Proposed:* TRY IT: THE BRICK TEST. Grab a paperclip and a timer. You have two minutes to write down as many uses for it as you can. Count your answers (that is your fluency), group them into categories (your flexibility), and star any you think nobody else would write (your originality). Psychologists have used this test, called the Alternative Uses Task, since the 1960s. Take the full version in the Creativity Test on our site: [site address].

*Why:* Links the book to the site's flagship tool (Creativity Test). Needs the site address (domain still undecided).

*Evidence:* Alternative Uses Task: J.P. Guilford, 1960s (citation to verify). It is the basis of the site's Creativity Test. *Confidence: High on the test; citation year to verify.*

**C1-10 (New technique, Core)**  
*Where:* After "So the rubbish bins of corporations everywhere..." (end of the Pyramid section, before the closing summary)

*Proposed:* TRY IT: THE PREMORTEM. Naysayers do most damage when they speak last. Psychologist Gary Klein's answer is to invite them in first. Before you pitch an idea, gather the team and say: "It is a year from now. The idea has failed spectacularly. Why?" Everyone writes down reasons in silence for three minutes, then reads them out. Klein described the method in Harvard Business Review in 2007. It builds on 1989 research suggesting that imagining an event has already happened helps people come up with more reasons for it, about 30 percent more in that study. Now the critics are part of the plan instead of the pyramid. Time: 15 minutes. Group: 4 or more. Materials: sticky notes and pens.

*Why:* Turns the Pyramid of the Powerless into something readers can use on Monday.

*Evidence:* Klein, "Performing a Project Premortem", Harvard Business Review, September 2007; Mitchell, Russo & Pennington (1989) on prospective hindsight (about 30% more reasons generated; check the primary paper). *Confidence: Medium (check the 1989 figure in the original paper).*

### Fact fixes and updates

**C1-12 (Fact fix, Core)**  
*Where:* "What's the big idea?", Edison paragraph

*Current:* For instance, take Thomas Edison's incandescent electric light, an idea he patented in 1879. (Actually, the first incandescent light source was developed by Humphry Davy in 1809.) It became the first 'arch lamp', which then became abbreviated to 'arc lamp' when the marketers got a hold of the idea. Attempts to roll out arc lamps commercially weren't successful because of the lack of a constant supply of electricity. It wasn't until Edison developed a system to supply electricity all over the place that the incandescent light became practical on a large scale. The point is, Davy's invention wasn't that useful until Edison found a way to make it so.

*Proposed:* For instance, take Thomas Edison's incandescent electric light, an idea he patented in 1879. (Actually, inventors had been making electric light for decades. Humphry Davy made a strip of platinum glow in 1802 and a few years later showed off a dazzling arc light at the Royal Institution.) Early electric lights never caught on, because nobody could supply a steady, affordable flow of electricity. It wasn't until Edison developed a whole system for delivering power that the incandescent light became practical on a large scale. The point is, Davy's invention wasn't that useful until Edison found a way to make it so.

*Why:* The 2010 text says the first incandescent light was Davy's in 1809 and that "arch lamp" became "arc lamp" through marketers. Davy's incandescence demo was 1802 and his arc lamp 1808-09 (sources differ); we found no source for the "arch lamp" story, so it is cut.

*Evidence:* Houston Engines of Our Ingenuity; Guinness World Records (arc lamp, Royal Institution, 1808); Open University. Sources disagree on 1808 or 1809 for the arc lamp; 1802 for the platinum strip. The "arch lamp became arc lamp" claim was not found in any source. *Confidence: Medium (arc lamp year differs by source; Edison patent dates not confirmed in this run).*
